The results demonstrated that adding 10 % RHA resulted in a remarkable improvement in compressive strength of 19.6 %, respectively, compared to conventional concrete. It may be due to RHA comprising a consid­ erable quantity of silica (SiO₂), which, when mixed with lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂) from cement, undergoes a pozzolanic reaction. This reaction produces additional calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H), which is the main compound responsible for the strength and durability of concrete. The formation of C-S-H improves the bond between parti­ cles and enhances the material’s mechanical properties, including flex­ ural strength. When 20 % RHA replaced OPC, the compressive strength decreased, yet it remained 6.9 % higher than the reference mix. The extra silica that RHA provides turns into an inert substance and doesn’t contribute to strength building when its content increases from 10 % to 20 %. This is because the hydration process doesn’t produce enough C-H to react with all of the silica that RHA makes available [67,68]. The additional silica provided by RHA can be identified as an inert material that does not aid in strength development. This occurs due to the insufficient quantity of C-H produced during the hydration process, which is inadequate to react with all the silica introduced by the RHA. The authors [27,29,69] observed similar outcomes. CR notably has a stronger effect in reducing compressive strength. However, 5 % and 10 % CR reduced compressive strength by 4.2 % and 23.9 %, respec­ tively.
