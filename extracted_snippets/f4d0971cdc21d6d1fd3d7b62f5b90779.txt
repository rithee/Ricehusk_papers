ANN model The concept of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), inspired by the neural networks in the human brain, was first developed by McCulloch and Pitts in 1943 [81]. ANNs are adept at modeling linear and nonlinear systems without the constraints often associated with traditional sta­ tistical methods. Typically, an ANN comprises three layers: an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. The training process of an ANN involves adjusting connection weights according to a learning rule derived from an input–output dataset, with the goal of minimizing the difference between predicted outputs and actual values [82]. This training process includes two main phases: feed-forward and back-propagation. During the feed-forward phase, data flows from the input layer through the hidden layers to the output layer. In the back- propagation phase, weights are adjusted from the output layer back to the input layer based on the overall error in the network. Traditionally, the evaluation of concrete’s compressive strength has depended on laboratory testing techniques, comprising crushing con­ crete samples with predetermined dimensions following a designated curing time. This method is universally accepted due to its ability to provide reliable measurements of a material’s load-bearing capacity [21]. Despite its widespread use, this approach is increasingly viewed as inefficient. The process is both time-intensive and costly, as it requires significant labor and specialized equipment. Moreover, it involves destructive testing, which does not allow for further use of the tested samples, thereby adding to material waste and cost. To overcome the limitations of traditional testing methods, some researchers have explored using empirical regression techniques to predict the CS of concrete based on its mix proportions [22–24]. These techniques involve creating mathematical models that estimate strength outcomes from the known quantities of the mixture’s ingredients, such as cement, water, aggregates, and admixtures. However, developing accurate regression models is challenging due to the intricate and nonlinear relationships among the various components of concrete. Factors such as the type and fineness of cement, the water-to-cement ratio, the size and quality of aggregates, and the presence of additives can all significantly influence the strength, making it difficult to predict outcomes precisely [25].
