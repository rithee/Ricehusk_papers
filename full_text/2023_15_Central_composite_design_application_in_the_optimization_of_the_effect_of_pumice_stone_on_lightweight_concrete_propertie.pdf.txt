A B S T R A C T   
Among other lightweight aggregates, one choice is pumice stone (PS), which can replace coarse 
aggregates in concrete. In this study, the natural coarse aggregates are replaced by various per­
centages of PS in concrete to examine its physical and mechanical properties. Experiments are 
designed to prepare mixtures (by varying ratios of PS) using response surface methodology 
(RSM). Slump and compaction factor tests are used to investigate the fresh properties, such as the 
workability of concrete. In contrast, the hardened concrete properties are determined using 
compressive strength (CS), flexural strength (FS), and split tensile strength (STS). The experi­
mental, optimization and validation of the model results show that up to 30% of the PS can be 
replaced in the lightweight aggregate concrete for 7 – 56 curing days in which the CS is greater 
than 15 MPa, STS is 7–12% of the CS, and FS is 9 – 11% of the CS. Therefore, the optimum CS for 
the current study ranges from 17 to 20 MPa, in which the STS is 9.39 – 10.54% of the CS, and FS 
ranges from 17.49% to 21.12% of the CS. The quadratic model was proposed, and the ANOVA and 
model fitness was produced through RSM, with the coefficient of determination (R2) above 0.99 
(99%) for all three responses representing the high importance of the suggested model. Besides, 
the difference among the R2 and adjusted R2 was negligible, and the P-Value for CS, STS, and FS 
was below 0.05 (<0.0001), indicating the model’s high relevance. RSM was used to improve the 
model, which was then verified in further lab trials.

main forms of modern concrete that have a specific role in the concrete mix design and are mostly generated from natural resources. 
Due to rapid infrastructure and development, concrete is used in abundance, degrading natural resources. Therefore, the researchers 
are now looking forward to reducing the use of natural coarse aggregates by replacing them at varying percentages [1,2] or fully [3] in

In recent and past studies, several industrial wastes are utilized in the concrete as a cement replacement, such as ash from timber 
[5], fly ash [6], glass polishing power [7], wood fiber waste [8], rice husk ash [9], limestone powder [10], demolition materials [11], 
calcium carbonate residues [12], and ceramic powder wastes [13]. However, some waste is used as a natural fine aggregate 
replacement, such as waste foundry sand [14], recycled glass [15], plastic [16], and rubber [17]. Besides, E-plastic waste [1], recycled 
concrete aggregate and ceramic waste [18], wood waste [19], and agro-waste [2] are used as a replacement for pure grainy aggregate 
in the concrete mix design and investigate the concrete properties. Regardless of the waste mentioned above, materials and industrial 
by-product waste, Lightweight Aggregate Pumice Stone (PS) are also highlighted in the literature as a replacement of pure coarse 
aggregate in concrete. 
Several studies are conducted on PS worldwide as a natural coarse aggregate replacement to investigate the strength properties, 
economic, and environmental benefits. Regarding the economic benefits, its vary from country to country. For instance, M. Saltan and 
F. Selcan Fındık (2004) conducted a study on the use of PS in flexible pavement and conclude the cost of 4.500$ per unit length for 
Turkey [20]. Besides, other researchers concluded ranging between $4 - $10 whereas natural stones are usually between $10 - $15. 
Using PS as a preferential substitute for natural coarse aggregate can provide environmental as well as economic benefits by suc­
cessfully utilizing waste resources that are available in abundant [21]. 
ACI 213R-87, 1998 defines lightweight structural concrete as concrete containing a density among 1350 and 1900 kg/m3 and 
minimum strength properties of 17 MPa. Over the last twenty years, there has been a significant increase in the understanding and 
implementation of high-strength, lightweight concrete based on artificial lightweight particles [22–24]. Pumice is a volcanic 
sponge-like substance formed by abruptly freezing magma and catching millions of microscopic air bubbles [25,26]. Pumice aggre­
gates are widespread on the edges of volcanic regions, notably in the Mediterranean region, the majority of Indonesia and Turkey, and 
the Rocky Mountains of the United States [27]. However, modest research has proven that structural concrete with strength properties 
up to 25 MPa may be manufactured with sufficient economic advantages [28–30]. 
Idi et al., and Sultan et al., also concluded that pumice stones have produced a slightly lower compressive strength associated with 
standard concrete meaning that there is a demand intended for additional materials to maintain the compressive strength at a lighter 
weight [21,31]. However, up to 50% replacement, the concrete incorporating PS can achieve 25 MPa strength and can be used for 
structural purposes whereas, beyond 50% replacement can be used for non-structural purposes. When lightweight construction 
concrete with a pumice stone is employed to build and maintain civil structural components, the advantages of lower total self-weight, 
fire resistance can be attained, decreased natural resource depletion (economic advantages), and improved sound insulation and heat 
[23]. Some researchers used the pumice stone as a fine aggregate replacement in mortar to evaluate the shrinkage (micro-cracks) in a 
mortar [32–35]. Liu et al. investigate the effect of pumices with varying water absorption rates used to substitute river sand by 10–30% 
on the qualities of the created Ultra-High Performance Concrete (UHPC). They discovered that using pre-wetting pumice (particle size 
distribution of 0.6–1.25 mm) may greatly minimize autogenous shrinkage of UHPC and improve the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) 
skeleton among UHPC paste and hydrous pumice while maintaining engineering properties [36]. 
Karthika et al. used pumice stone in concrete as a coarse aggregate at a varying replacement ratio to evaluate the strength per­
formance. They concluded that by using 50% of pumice lightweight aggregate concrete (PLWAC), the optimum CS, STS, and FS can be 
achieved and reduces the self-weight and absorbs more water due to the pores, which reduce the strength properties that can be used 
for non-load bearing walls, partition walls, and earthquake resistance structures [37]. Besides, Zhang and Gjvorv focus on the strength 
controlling factor of high-strength lightweight concrete (HSLWC) to investigate the mechanical properties by using five different types 
of lightweight aggregates. They concluded that the STS/CS ratio is lower for HSLWC than HSNWC. The elastic modulus varies from 
17.8 to 25.9 MPa and is much lower than HSNWC [38]. T. Parhizkar et.al. investigated the structural cost, dead load reduction, 
foundation size, and reinforcement by using a pumice stone to partially replace coarse aggregate [39]. B. Devi Pravallika and K. 
Venkateswara Rao recommended improving the durability and strength attributes of M40 concrete by substituting coarse aggregate 
(0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%) with a natural lightweight aggregate pumice stone [40]. C. Krishnaveni and K. Guru Kesav 
Kumar investigated the use of pumice stone as a partial aggregate substitute in cement concrete [41]. Several studies have been 
conducted on the waste replacement, by-product, and replacement of natural resources over other abundantly available natural re­
sources to evaluate fresh, mechanical, and microstructural properties [42–48]. 
Response surface modeling (RSM) is a collection of computational and scientific methods that are used to examine the interplay of 
several exogenous variables and the response variable (s) of a physical system. Central Composite Design (CCD) is used in the design 
expert software to design and optimize the experiments. After getting the mix design from the RSM – CCD, the designed experiments 
are performed. The experimental values are used as input parameters for the linear regression to build the statistical model. The curing 
days and percentage replacement are used as input parameters, and the strength properties such as CS, STS, and FS were set as response 
variables. Several researchers used RSM for their experimental design and optimization in recent studies. For instance, Zahid et al. used 
RSM to predict the engineering properties of geopolymer composite by replacing it with Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC). Ali et al. 
used the design and statistical modeling of concrete incorporating Waste Foundry Sand (WFS) as a sand replacement to evaluate the 
strength properties [49]. Khan et al. used the partial substitution of OPC by silica fume and irradiated waste polyethylene terephthalate 
in various quantities to evaluate the impact on semi-flexible payment [16,50]. Moreover, Al Salaheen et al. used response surface 
methods to estimate and optimize mortar compressive strength by using heat-treated fly oil shale ash as an effective supplemental

Based on the recent and relevant literature, it is vital to examine the influence of natural coarse aggregate as a partial and full

Pumice is a light-colored porous igneous rock created through explosive volcanic eruptions when molten lava containing many gas 
bubbles is hurled into the air. When it cools, it hardens into pumice. Pumice stone (PS) has numerous physical properties. It became an 
extraordinarily light rock due to the many permeable bubbles/holes inside it. With an average porosity of 90%, PS are lightweight and 
initially float in water [58]. The density of pumice varies according to the thickness of the solid substance between the bubbles. It’s 
used in various industrial and consumer products as a lightweight concrete aggregate, landscaping material, and abrasive [59]. The PS 
was gathered from one of the US apparel industries that are located in Lahore city of Pakistan as shown in Fig. 4.

deposit, but it is mostly composed of aluminum oxide, silicon dioxide (amorphous aluminum silicate), and residue volumes of 
additional oxides. The pumice stone has the largest SiO2 content compared to the other constituents, and it is a mixture of Si, Al, Ca, 
Mg, and Fe oxides [60]. The primary constituents, such as SiO2, Al2O3, and Na2O, constitute 87.78% of the total, while the remaining 
constituents provide 12.22%.

2.2. Concrete mix design 
The PS was substituted as a coarse aggregate with varying percentages of 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 75%, and 100%, producing a 
total of seven different mixtures. Depending on the trials, a sum of 189 specimens being produced and prepared in twenty-one (21) 
cylinders for CS on seven days, with three samples created for 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 75%, and 100% (to take the average value for 
the specific specimen to avoid human and experimental errors) which makes a total of twenty-one cylinders. The same procedure was 
followed for 28 and 56 days of curing. To test the FS and STS of lightweight concrete using PS, twenty-one cylinders were produced and 
arranged for the same proportion and amount of curing days. The cylinder mold size utilized for concrete casting was 6" x 12" for the CS 
and STS, and 150 × 150×500 mm for the FS. Fig. 5(a) depicts the casting and coding of the cylinder, whereas Fig. 5(b) shows the 
curing of a cylinder containing PS. 
2.3. Compressive strength (CS) 
CS tests on concrete specimens should be performed using a universal testing machine (UTM) to evaluate the load-carrying 
capability under compression. The bottom and top sides of the column specimen are sealed with plaster of Paris with a depth of 12 
millimeters to adequately disperse the stress. A steel plate with a 9"x9" cross-sectional area is also put just on the upper portion of the 
column sample to ensure consistent load distribution. The specimen is positioned among the jars such that the filled face approaches 
the testing equipment. Vertical loads are applied at a constant rate till failure happens, and the highest load reading at the failure stage 
is reported. The CS test is performed using the ASTM C39 standard test procedure [61].

2.6. Water absorption 
The water absorption is determined based on ASTM C 642–97. Water absorption of natural concrete specimens is lower compared 
to that of lightweight aggregate concrete (PS) samples. The high moisture content of lightweight aggregates at SSD circumstances 
causes a greater water/cement proportion in the transition zone (among paste and aggregates), resulting in enhanced permeability of 
the concretes. 
2.7. Fresh concrete workability 
The properly/improperly mixing batch can be accessed through the consistency and workability of concrete. The slump test is used 
to determine the consistency of fresh concrete components before they harden. Slump value and compaction factors are events that 
may be used to assess the workability of fresh concrete and, as a result, the speed with which concrete moves.

3.1. Slump cone test 
A slump cone test determines the fresh properties of lightweight aggregate concrete incorporating PS. The slump cone test is a 
popular method for determining the consistency of concrete. The experimental results show that the slump values decrease by 
increasing the percentage of the PS in lightweight aggregate concrete as natural coarse aggregate. The decrease in the slump values is 
maybe due to the regular shape and size of the PS. The wetter the mix, the higher will be the slump values [64]. Due to the proper shape 
of PS, the air voids or air entrained are properly filled up which causes in reduction of slump values. The highest slump value is found 
on the 10% replacement of the PS. Table 4 and Fig. 6 depict the slump values obtained at varying replacement percentages. The 
coefficient of determination (R2) is 0.984 showing a 98.4% correlation between the slump values and percentage replacement. 
3.2. Water absorption 
The water absorption assessment is used to determine how much water the concrete absorbs. This test is performed to determine 
the permeability of concrete. The absorption rate of the PS-containing concrete is greater than that of the control mix with 0% PS 
substitution as a natural coarse aggregate. However, there is a variation in the absorption of the concrete containing PS at varying 
percentages due to their internal properties. The highest water absorption was found at 50% replacement of the PS in concrete. 
Karthika et al. concluded that the highest water absorption was found at 50% PLWAC, and beyond that, it was decreased [37], whereas 
Kale et al. investigated that the increase of PS in concrete caused a rise in water absorption of concrete due to its pores [65]. The fall 
and rise in the water absorption of concrete incorporating PS are due to the microstructure properties of the PS. The aggregate used in 
the current study is in wet conditions with surface moisture of 3.5%. Furthermore, the moisture level of the aggregate must be assessed 
earlier to mixing because it influences the quantity of mixed water necessary to attain the requisite water/cementitious materials ratio 
[66].

3.5. Flexural strength (FS) 
As per the ASTM C293, the FS of lightweight aggregate concrete including PS as a natural coarse aggregate substitute for 7–56 days 
of drying was investigated [63]. Compared to the concrete’s STS, the FS of the concrete is essential. Fig. 16 depicts the experimental 
results of FS containing PS at varying percentages and curing days. Although the number of specimens and the curing days for the FS of 
concrete are similar to STS and CS, the beam’s dimension varied. The prism beam was used to produce the samples for the FS with a 
dimension of 150 × 150×500 mm. 
As the proportion of the PS rises, the strength properties decrease; however, up to 30% substitute, the CS, STS, and FS for 7–56 days 
are within the ranges of LWA strength. The strength properties have a direct relationship with the curing days, increase in curing days 
can increase the strength properties. The highest was recorded on 56 days, which means that replacing the PS as natural coarse 
aggregate improves the FS but the opposite in CS. The FS on 7–56 days of drying with a 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 75%, and 100% 
replacement are within and greater than the standard limits for conventional concrete. Banawair et al., write a review on the strength 
properties of LWAC and show the relationship among FS and CS, where they concluded that the ratio of FS to CS is 11.6–13.5% 
whereas, the medium and high strength LWAC is typically within the range among 9–11% of the compressive strength [74]. 
In the current study, the ratio between CS and FS ranges from 16.65% to 25.03% for 7 days of curing and 18.63–26.38% on 28 days. 
However, for the 56 days of curing, it ranges from 19.46% to 26.76%. Therefore, this study concludes that the ratio of the CS to FS is 
increased by rising the proportion substitution of PS in the LWAC, whereas FS is the opposite. Lakshman et al., and Minapu et al., also 
achieved that the FS of concrete increases by increasing the replacement of natural aggregate with PS [75,76].

5. Conclusion 
The current study aims to replace the lightweight aggregate PS at a varying percentage as natural coarse aggregate to evaluate the 
strength properties of concrete incorporating PS at 7, 28, and 56 curing days. The statistical analysis and optimization of the RSM were 
used for the experimental design to design and execute the experiments based on the proposed run from RSM by adjusting the upper 
and lower boundaries of the drying days and PS percentage replacement as input parameters. The output parameters of the RSM were 
the strength properties such as STS, FS, and CS. After the laboratory experiments, the data is entered into RSM to generate a statistical 
equation, ANOVA, predicted vs. actual, contour, and 3D graphs for all the curing days and all the outputs, such as STS, FS, and CS. 
Below is a summary of the current study that is concluded as follows.  
• There is a slight variation in the fresh properties of lightweight aggregate concrete incorporating PS. As the percentage of the PS 
increase, the slump values decreases. The slump cone test findings show that pumice lightweight aggregate concrete with 10% 
replacement has higher workability than the other replacement percentages. However, there is an increase and decrease in the 
water absorption properties of concrete containing PS due to the microstructure and internal properties of PS. In the current study, 
the highest water absorption was found on 50% replacement which is in line with the Kartika et al. studies. 
• In terms of mechanical properties, the control mix with 0% replacement shows a high CS, STS, and FS than the rest of the per­
centages for all the curing days. However, this study concluded that concrete with up to 30% replacement of lightweight aggregate
