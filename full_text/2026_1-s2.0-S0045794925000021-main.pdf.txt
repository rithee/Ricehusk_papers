A B S T R A C T
Compressive strength is a key factor in the design and durability of concrete structures. Accurate prediction of 
compressive strength helps optimize material use and reduce construction costs. This study proposes a novel 
stacked model for predicting compressive strength, integrating three base models with linear regression. The 
base models include Artificial Neural Networks, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting, while the 
stacked model uses Linear Regression as the metamodel. A dataset of 1,030 concrete mix samples covering eight 
critical input parameters, including cement, blast furnace slag, coarse aggregates, fine aggregates, fly ash, water, 
superplasticizer, and curing days, was used for training and evaluation. The dataset was split into training (80%), 
validation (10%), and testing (10%) subsets. The models were trained independently, and their predictions were 
used to develop the stacked model. Among the base models, the Extreme Gradient Boosting model achieved the 
highest accuracy, with an R2 of 0.947 during testing. However, the stacked model outperformed it, attaining an 
R2 of 0.953 in the testing phase. Shapley additive explanations analysis identified curing duration as the most 
influential factor in compressive strength prediction. A user-friendly graphical interface was developed to 
facilitate efficient prediction of compressive strength in concrete structures.
1. Introduction
The increasing release of greenhouse-gases like carbon dioxide, 
methane, and nitrous oxide into the atmosphere, primarily driven by 
human activities such as burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and indus­
trial processes, is causing profound changes to the Earth’s environment. 
This rise leads to widespread climate disruptions, including shifts in 
weather patterns, more frequent extreme weather events, and severe 
impacts on ecosystems [1]. A particularly visible consequence is the 
reduction of polar ice stores in the Arctic and Antarctic regions, where 
ice caps and glaciers are melting at an accelerating rate. This reduction 
poses significant ecological challenges, including rising sea levels and 
disrupted ecosystems, which threaten biodiversity and the livelihoods of 
millions of people worldwide. Among the contributors to these emis­
sions, the building industry, a key sector within the global economy, 
emerges as the largest contributor to greenhouse-gas emissions, ac­
counting for nearly 50 % of the total worldwide emissions. This sub­
stantial impact underscores the industry’s pivotal role in global 
environmental concerns [2]. This is primarily due to the extensive use of 
concrete, which relies heavily on Portland cement (PC) as a key 
component [3]. PC is a major driver of CO2 emissions, accounting for 
approximately 7 % of the total global emissions [4]. The production 
process of PC involves the calcination of limestone (calcium carbonate), 
which releases carbon dioxide. Notably, the calcination process, where 
calcium oxide (CaO) is produced, is responsible for a significant portion 
of these emissions. As urbanization and infrastructure development 
continue to expand, the demand for concrete and consequently PC has 
surged. Presently, the annual production of PCs stands at 4,000 million 
tons, with forecasts predicting this figure will rise to 6,000 million tons 
by 2060 [5]. This trend exacerbates environmental concerns and high­
lights the need for sustainable alternatives. Addressing this critical issue 
requires the development of effective substitutes that can satisfy the 
increasing demand while drastically lowering energy consumption and 
CO2 emissions [6]. One promising approach is incorporating waste and 
recycled materials into concrete production. This method not only ad­
dresses the demand for construction materials but also contributes to 
reducing environmental hazards and conserving natural resources. By 
diverting industrial waste from landfills and utilizing it in concrete, we 
can mitigate the ecological footprint of construction activities [7,8].
Various industrial by-products have been recognized as suitable al­
ternatives to PC in concrete production. Some of the most promising

significant amounts during industrial activities and often present chal­

Concrete remains the most widely used construction material glob­
ally, surpassed only by water consumption [14–17]. Its widespread 
availability, malleability upon cooling, and strength to support loads 
after hardening are reasons for its widespread acclaim. These properties 
make concrete an essential material for various construction applica­
tions. The performance of concrete, particularly its compressive 
strength, is critical for the safety and durability of structures [18]. 
Compressive strength (CS) refers to the material’s ability to withstand 
loads that compress or reduce its size. This property is influenced by 
various factors, including the water-to-binder ratio, the size and quality 
of aggregates, and the type of binder used [19]. The heterogeneous 
nature of concrete, which comprises a mix of binder, aggregates, water, 
and admixtures, adds to the complexity of predicting its compressive 
strength. Each component and how they are combined can significantly 
affect the final properties of the concrete. For example, a lower water-to- 
binder ratio can lead to higher compressive strength but may reduce 
workability, while aggregates’ size and grading can impact the finished 
product’s density and durability. The type of binder, whether it is a 
traditional PC or an alternative material, also plays a crucial role in 
determining the concrete’s performance [20].
Traditionally, the evaluation of concrete’s compressive strength has 
depended on laboratory testing techniques, comprising crushing con­
crete samples with predetermined dimensions following a designated 
curing time. This method is universally accepted due to its ability to 
provide reliable measurements of a material’s load-bearing capacity 
[21]. Despite its widespread use, this approach is increasingly viewed as 
inefficient. The process is both time-intensive and costly, as it requires 
significant labor and specialized equipment. Moreover, it involves 
destructive testing, which does not allow for further use of the tested 
samples, thereby adding to material waste and cost. To overcome the 
limitations of traditional testing methods, some researchers have 
explored using empirical regression techniques to predict the CS of 
concrete based on its mix proportions [22–24]. These techniques involve 
creating mathematical models that estimate strength outcomes from the 
known quantities of the mixture’s ingredients, such as cement, water, 
aggregates, and admixtures. However, developing accurate regression 
models is challenging due to the intricate and nonlinear relationships 
among the various components of concrete. Factors such as the type and 
fineness of cement, the water-to-cement ratio, the size and quality of 
aggregates, and the presence of additives can all significantly influence 
the strength, making it difficult to predict outcomes precisely [25].

In the context of concrete technology, ML models can be trained on 
large datasets of past concrete mix compositions and their correspond­
ing compressive strengths, allowing for the creation of predictive models 
that can forecast the strength of new mixtures with high accuracy. 
Machine learning offers several advantages over traditional empirical 
regression methods, particularly in handling the complexities and non- 
linearities inherent in concrete mixture design. ML models can learn 
from vast amounts of data and identify subtle patterns that might not be 
evident through conventional methods. This ability to model complex 
relationships makes ML particularly useful for predicting the CS of 
concrete, including variations with alternative materials like fly ash. 
Furthermore, ML models can adapt and improve over time as more data 
becomes available, continually refining their predictions.
Fly ash, a by-product of coal combustion has emerged as a valuable 
alternative material in concrete production due to its environmental and 
performance benefits. Incorporating fly ash into concrete not only helps 
in waste management by reducing the need for landfill space but also 
improves the mechanical properties of concrete, such as its durability 
and resistance to chemical attack. Using ML models to predict the CS of 
fly ash-based concrete is essential for optimizing mix designs. Accurate 
predictions ensure that the concrete meets necessary performance 
standards while also maximizing the use of sustainable materials. This 
approach helps in achieving a balance between structural integrity and 
environmental sustainability. Various factors affect the CS of concrete, 
such as the water-to-binder ratio, the size and quality of aggregates, the 
type of binder employed, the curing period, and the distinct properties of 
alternative materials like fly ash. The water-to-binder ratio, for instance, 
directly affects the porosity of the concrete, which in turn influences its 
strength and durability. Similarly, aggregates’ size and grading can 
impact the mix’s workability and CS. Understanding these factors and 
their interactions is critical for designing concrete mixtures that meet 
specific strength and performance criteria.
Implementing ML in analysing large datasets from past concrete 
mixtures allows for developing models that accurately predict concrete 
characteristics. By examining historical data, ML models can optimize 
the proportions of materials in new mixtures, enhancing quality control 
and consistency in concrete production. These models are particularly 
effective in identifying the optimal balance of materials that achieve 
desired strength properties while minimizing costs and environmental 
impact. The use of ML in this context improves the efficiency of concrete 
design and supports the development of more sustainable building 
practices. ML models play a pivotal role in promoting the sustainability 
of concrete mix designs. ML can help reduce the environmental footprint 
of concrete production by optimising material combinations. For 
instance, using supplementary cementitious materials like fly ash can 
lower carbon emissions associated with cement production, significantly 
contributing to global CO2 emissions. Additionally, optimizing the mix 
design can reduce material waste and improve concrete structures’ 
overall lifespan and durability, leading to long-term sustainability 
benefits.
The application of ML in structural engineering has gained signifi­
cant attention, particularly in predicting the concrete’s CS. Various 
studies have demonstrated the potential of ML models in this domain. 
For instance, Chithra et al. [53] successfully utilized Artificial Neural 
Networks (ANN) to predict the CS of high-performance concrete (HPC) 
containing silica nanoparticles and copper slag, showcasing the ANN 
model’s ability to handle complex material properties. Similarly, 
Nguyen et al. [54] developed four different ML models to forecast the 
compressive and tensile strength of HPC, with their findings empha­
sizing the superior accuracy of Gradient-Boosting-Regressor (GBR) and 
Extreme-Gradient-Boosting (XGB) models in capturing intricate data 
patterns. Kumar et al. [55], focusing on lightweight concrete (LWC), 
employed SVM models based on data from 120 experimental sets, 
highlighting SVM’s effectiveness in evaluating LWC compressive 
strength across diverse datasets. In another study, Ashrafian [56]
applied heuristic regression methods to analyze the strength and ultra­
sonic pulse velocity of fibrous concrete, providing valuable insights into 
the material’s behavior under various conditions. Using a comprehen­
sive investigation of eight input factors crucial to the model’s accuracy, 
Zhang et al. [57] employed the Random Forest (RF) approach to forecast 
the uniaxial CS of lightweight self-compacting concrete (LSC).
Furthermore, the incorporation of nanotechnology, particularly 
Nanosilica, has also been explored to enhance the compressive strength 
of concrete, with various models assessing the influence of mix pro­
portions and curing conditions on the material’s strength [58]. Aslam 
et al. [59] developed a GEP model to predict the compressive strength of 
high-strength concrete (HSC), leveraging a comprehensive dataset of 
357 points to illustrate GEP’s robustness in modeling complex material 
characteristics. Collectively, these studies underscore the diverse ap­
plications and effectiveness of various ML models in predicting concrete 
properties, highlighting the significant advancements in this field. The 
overview of these ML models and approaches used in past literature is 
further detailed in Table 1, providing a comprehensive understanding of 
the advancements in this field.
2. Research significance
The novelty of this research resides in its thorough methodology for 
using machine learning models to predict CS and developing an inno­
vative stacked linear regression model (SM-LR), which provides sub­
stantial enhancements in accuracy and practical usability compared to 
conventional techniques. This model integrated three ML models: ANN, 
RF, and XGB, as the base models. These models were chosen based on 
their strong predictive capabilities, which, combined with stacking, can 
effectively overcome the weaknesses of each model. These selected 
models align better with the dataset’s size and complexity, avoiding the 
need for extensive data, which deep learning models require. They strike 
a balance between predictive performance and the ability to interpret 
how inputs affect outputs, which is crucial for applications in structural 
engineering where model insights guide decisions. Given the available 
computational resources, these models offer a practical compromise 
between accuracy and computational demands. The literature review

3. Research objectives
This study extends prior research by utilizing ML models to predict 
the CS of concrete within a data range (2 to 82 MPa). The primary aim 
was to assess the effectiveness of various base ML models in predicting 
CS and then enhance their accuracy by stacking techniques. The 
research methodology, illustrated in Fig. 1, involved collecting and 
analyzing data from 1030 datasets, including parameters such as cement 
and aggregates, with the properties examined through histograms and 
heatmaps. This study utilized three ML models to predict the CS of 
concrete. The models’ effectiveness was assessed by comparing the 
actual values with the predicted outcomes, employing various regres­
sion metrics. To ensure reliable results, k-fold cross-validation was used 
throughout the evaluation process. Additionally, an in-depth sensitivity 
analysis was performed to explore how different variables affect model 
accuracy. The findings were compared with prior studies to pinpoint the 
most accurate model. This methodology presents a more efficient and 
economically viable alternative to conventional experimental proced­
ures, offering deeper insights into the factors influencing concrete CS 
prediction.
4. Methodology
4.1. Database collection
Using information gathered from previous studies or data collected 
in the lab, researchers are able to develop predictive models. This study 
conducted a review of previous academic literature, including notable 
works by Song et al. [19,60] and Yeh [78], to compile a dataset 
comprising 1,030 data points pertinent to the CS of concrete. The pri­
mary features used as input variables in the analysis included cement 
(C), blast furnace slag (Slag), fly ash (FA), water (W), superplasticizer 
(SP), coarse aggregate (Cagg), fine aggregate (Fagg), and the curing 
duration (Age). These factors were crucial in determining the final 
compressive strength outcome.

4.6. Description of base models
4.6.1. ANN model
The concept of Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), inspired by the 
neural networks in the human brain, was first developed by McCulloch 
and Pitts in 1943 [81]. ANNs are adept at modeling linear and nonlinear 
systems without the constraints often associated with traditional sta­
tistical methods. Typically, an ANN comprises three layers: an input 
layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. The training 
process of an ANN involves adjusting connection weights according to a 
learning rule derived from an input–output dataset, with the goal of 
minimizing the difference between predicted outputs and actual values 
[82]. This training process includes two main phases: feed-forward and 
back-propagation. During the feed-forward phase, data flows from the 
input layer through the hidden layers to the output layer. In the back- 
propagation phase, weights are adjusted from the output layer back to 
the input layer based on the overall error in the network. To further 
reduce this error, an appropriate back-propagation algorithm is 
employed, with the Levenberg-Marquardt technique being the most 
efficient and commonly used method for this purpose. Fig. 5 shows that 
the eight variables were used as inputs to the ANN model, and the output 
variable Y, which represents the concrete CS, was set as the outcome.
4.6.2. RF model
The Random Forest (RF) model, initially introduced by Ho [83] and 
further developed by Breiman [84], is an advanced ensemble learning 
method that utilizes multiple decision trees to enhance prediction ac­
curacy. This technique applies bootstrap resampling to create various 
instances from the original dataset, with each instance used to construct 
an individual decision tree [85]. Predictions from these trees are then 
averaged to produce the final output. RF improves on the limitations of 
traditional decision trees, such as susceptibility to overfitting, by 
incorporating randomness in the selection of parameters during the tree 
construction. This not only makes the model more robust by combining 
well-established trees but also balances the weaknesses and strengths 
across different classes. The model structures condition hierarchically 
from the root to the terminal nodes, with decision-making processes at 
each node and a simple regression model at each terminal node to make 
predictions. Pruning is applied to reduce complexity and enhance the 
model’s generalization capabilities. Fig. 6 shows the RF model’s

4.7. Hyperparameters tuning
In this study, Bayesian Optimization (BO) is employed to optimize 
hyperparameter settings, offering a more efficient alternative to tradi­
tional methods like Grid Search (GS) and Random Search (RS) [88]. 
Unlike GS, which exhaustively searches the entire hyperparameter 
space, BO models the objective function’s prior distribution and refines 
the search to identify the optimal configuration. The BO process begins 
with defining the hyperparameter ranges and initial distributions for 
each model, aiming to maximize performance within these predefined 
bounds. By reducing unnecessary trials, BO accelerates the identification 
of the most effective hyperparameter combinations, significantly 
enhancing tuning efficiency [89]. Critical hyperparameters, such as the 
number of estimators, learning rate, maximum tree depth, and regula­
rization parameters, were optimized using BO. This systematic approach 
minimizes trial-and-error, ensuring that the models achieve optimal 
performance. The selected configurations were validated on the test 
dataset to confirm their predictive efficacy.
Furthermore, this research integrates k-fold cross-validation (CV) to 
assess model robustness and mitigate overfitting. In particular, a 10-fold 
CV is utilized, where the dataset is partitioned into ten subsets, with 
each serving as the test set while the remaining nine are used for 
training. This method, known for balancing computational efficiency 
and the bias-variance trade-off, was combined with BO for hyper­
parameter optimization (BO + 10CV). The use of a 10-fold CV ensures 
accurate performance estimation while maintaining manageable 
computational demands.
4.8. Description of the stacked linear regression model
Stacking is an ensemble learning technique designed to enhance the 
predictive accuracy of machine learning models by combining multiple 
individual models [90,91]. The fundamental idea is to train several base 
models (BMs), often referred to as level-0 models, on the same dataset 
and then use another model, known as the meta-model or level-1 model, 
to aggregate their predictions into a final prediction. In a stacking 
ensemble, multiple BMs are trained independently on the training data. 
These models make predictions on the validation dataset, generating 
several forecasts. These predictions form a new dataset, where each 
column represents the predictions from a different base model. The 
meta-model is then trained on this new dataset to learn how to combine 
the base model predictions into a final output.
When linear regression is used as the meta-model in a stacking 
ensemble, the process starts with training the base models: ANN, RF, and 
XGB on the training dataset. These BMs make predictions on the vali­
dation dataset, creating a new dataset composed of these predictions. A 
linear regression model is trained on this new dataset, using the actual 
target values from the validation data as the response variable. The 
linear regression model learns to weigh the contributions of each base 
model’s predictions to produce a final, combined prediction.
Stacking with a linear regression meta-model can significantly 
improve model performance by leveraging the strengths of various BMs. 
This method allows for flexibility in model choice and can capture 
diverse patterns in the data, making the final model more robust and 
accurate. Additionally, by combining multiple models, stacking reduces 
the risk of overfitting, as the meta-model balances the strengths and 
weaknesses of each base model. By employing stacking with linear 
regression, we achieve a more accurate and robust predictive model that 
benefits from the diverse insights provided by the base models. This 
approach is particularly effective in complex datasets where no single 
model performs optimally.

5. Results and discussions
5.1. Optimal hyperparameters of developed BMs
Hyperparameters play a crucial role in the performance and effi­
ciency of machine learning models. Unlike model parameters, which are 
learned from the data during training, hyperparameters are set prior to 
the training process and guide the learning process. The selection of 
appropriate hyperparameters can significantly influence the model’s 
ability to generalize and make accurate predictions on unseen data. For 
the ANN, the optimal configuration includes three hidden layers, each 
with 20 neurons. Both the hidden layers and the output layer use the 
sigmoid activation function, which helps achieve non-linearity and en­
sures the model can capture complex patterns in the data.
The RF model, optimized for this study, consists of 313 estimators, 
with each tree having a maximum depth of 23. The model uses a min­
imum samples split of 2.0 and a minimum samples leaf of 1.0, ensuring 
that each split and leaf has a sufficient number of samples to avoid 
overfitting. The XGB model is fine-tuned with 493 estimators and a 
learning rate of 0.067, balancing the speed of learning and convergence. 
The model’s maximum depth is set to 4.0, allowing it to capture in­
teractions between features. Additionally, a column sample with tree 
value of 0.724 and a sub-sample value of 0.40 are used to prevent 
overfitting by adding randomness to the model training process. These 
optimized hyperparameters enhance the performance and accuracy of 
the respective ML models, enabling them to deliver robust and reliable 
predictions.

5.6. Feature importance and interpretability
5.6.1. SHAP analysis
The purpose of this sensitivity analysis was to assess the impact of 
individual input features on the output variable in the prediction of 
concrete compressive strength. The SHAP feature importance method 
was utilized to analyze the impact of each input variable during the 
testing stage based on the most effective predictive base ML model (i.e., 
XGB model). Fig. 16 presents the results, involving both dot and sum­
mary bar plots that offer significant insights into feature importance. 
The interpretations of the SHAP analysis are as follows: 
• Inputs are ordered by importance, with the most important inputs at 
the top.
• The position of the dots along the x-axis shows the impact of the 
input on the concrete’s CS value. The positive values indicate a 
positive impact, while the negative values indicate a negative effect.
• Inputs with longer bars exert a greater influence on the output of the 
model.
• Color coding facilitates the comprehension of the input’s value by 
assigning blue to low values and red to high values.
Fig. 16a illustrates a comprehensive SHAP dot plot, with each dot 
denoting a SHAP value corresponding to a particular instance within the 
dataset. The figure demonstrates that the curing age (Age) exerts the 
most significant influence on the concrete CS, with values ranging from 
−100 to 250. The cement content (C) shows a comparable trend to 
curing age, serving as the second critical input, which highlights its 
advantageous impact on the enhancement of concrete strength. The 
SHAP values exhibit a range from −75 to 100, where elevated values 
signify enhanced concrete strength. The inputs fine aggregate (Fagg) 
and blast furnace slag (Slag) exhibit a moderate influence, as indicated 
by SHAP values that are less dispersed compared to those of curing age 
and cement content. The SHAP values range from −60 to 70 for the Fagg 
variable and from −45 to 50 for the Slag variable. In contrast, the 
Superplasticizer (SP) exhibits a less pronounced effect, as indicated by 
SHAP values that cluster near zero, with a range from −25 to 20, 
implying a minimal influence on CS values. The coarse aggregate (Cagg) 
and fly ash (FA) exhibit neutral to weak effects on the model’s pre­
dictions, as indicated by SHAP values clustering near zero, which im­
plies a minimal influence on compressive strength within the analyzed 
range of values. Lastly, water (W) exhibits a less pronounced effect on 
the model’s predictions, as indicated by SHAP values that are predom­
inantly clustered around zero. This suggests that although they can 
impact concrete strength, their influence is less significant in compari­
son to other variables.
In addition, Fig. 16b indicates that curing age is the primary variable 
affecting the CS of concrete, with cement content and fine aggregates 
following as secondary and third factors, respectively. The curing age 
directly influences the hardening process of concrete, whereas the 
cement content is essential for bonding the mix. Consequently, these two 
factors are pivotal in predicting strength. Slag and fly ash, while sig­
nificant, provide a more moderate influence, generally contributing to 
the enhancement of long-term strength and durability. Coarse aggre­
gates contribute significantly, albeit to a lesser extent, while the impact 
of water and superplasticizers is minimal. The low ranking of water 
indicates that other factors, such as cement content, mediate its influ­
ence. The limited impact of superplasticizers highlights their function in 
enhancing workability rather than directly affecting strength. The pri­
mary factors influencing concrete strength in this model are curing age, 
cement content, and aggregate properties.

5.7. Comparison with previous studies
The developed SM-LR was evaluated against recent studies using ML 
and deep learning (DL) approaches to predict concrete CS. As shown in 
Fig. 18, a comparative analysis with previous research on CS prediction 
is presented. Liu [106] achieved an exceptionally high accuracy with an 
R2-value of 0.999 using the XGB model. However, it should be noted that 
this result was obtained with a relatively small dataset of 60 samples, 
which may not generalize well to larger datasets. Liang et al. [107] and 
Hongwei et al. [19] conducted similar studies using smaller datasets of 
190 and 98 samples, respectively. Their best models achieved high R2- 
values (0.947 and 0.950, respectively) but had higher RMSE and MAE 
values. Satish et al. [108] developed an XGB model with better perfor­
mance on a moderately large dataset of 633 samples, achieving an R2- 
value of 0.950, RMSE of 3.06 MPa, and MAE of 2.13 MPa.
In this research, the SM-LR model achieved an R2-value of 0.953, an 
RMSE of 3.315 MPa, and an MAE of 2.493 MPa, demonstrating excep­
tional performance on a large dataset of 1030 samples. Additionally, 
Feng et al. [109] developed an Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost) model on 
the same dataset, achieving an R2 of 0.940, RMSE of 1.93 MPa, and MAE 
of 1.43 MPa. Elhishi et al. [110] reported an R2 of 0.910, RMSE of 4.37 
MPa, and MAE of 3.04 MPa, indicating a lower correlation and higher 
error metrics compared to the SM-LR model. In summary, the SM-LR 
model in this study outperformed similar models from other studies 
with comparable dataset sizes, particularly in terms of the R2-value.

7. Limitations and future work
While the developed model demonstrates strong predictive perfor­
mance, several limitations should be noted. The model’s effectiveness 
heavily relies on the quality and representativeness of the input data. 
Since the dataset used in this study does not include key factors such as 
temperature, humidity, and curing methods, its ability to generalize to 
other environments or experimental setups may be limited. Further­
more, the model does not account for all variables that may influence 
concrete CS, such as specific mix proportions and external environ­
mental conditions, potentially affecting its accuracy in certain real- 
world scenarios. Additionally, while the GUI is designed for simplicity, 
users must still have an understanding of the input parameters and their 
relevance to make the most effective use of the tool.
Future work should focus on expanding the dataset to incorporate a 
broader range of concrete compositions, environmental conditions, and 
laboratory variables, including those that might affect the curing pro­
cess. Moreover, integrating advanced deep learning models could 
enhance predictive accuracy by identifying more complex relationships 
in the data. The GUI could also be improved by adding interactive fea­
tures, guidance, and tutorials to help users with varying levels of 
expertise navigate the input process. Finally, validating the model using 
real-world data from diverse applications and environmental conditions 
will be crucial to ensure its robustness, generalizability, and practical 
applicability.
