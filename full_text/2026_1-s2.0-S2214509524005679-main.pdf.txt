A B S T R A C T   
Performance assessment of existing building structures, especially concrete compressive strength 
assessment, is a crucial aspect of engineering construction for most industrialized countries. Non- 
destructive testing (NDT) techniques are commonly employed to assess the compressive strength 
of concrete structures. However, existing methods for predicting concrete compressive strength 
using NDT techniques and machine learning methods do not take into account the concrete mix 
proportion design. This study proposes an effective method to predict concrete compressive 
strength by combining NDT tests with different mix proportion designs and curing ages. Specif­
ically, support vector machine regression (SVR) and back propagation neural network (BPNN) 
models are established. Furthermore, various machine learning evaluation indexes are utilized to 
assess the model performance. To construct and validate the prediction models, a total of 180 
datasets containing concrete specimens with different mix proportion designs and curing ages are 
collected from the existing research literature. The prediction results show that the coefficients of 
determination (R2) of the SVR and the BPNN prediction models for the test set of concrete 
compressive strength are 86.0 % and 86.7 % without considering the concrete mix proportion 
design. The R2 of the prediction results of the SVR model is higher than 95 % when considering 
the effects of concrete mix proportion design and curing age. The R2 of the BPNN prediction 
model ranged between 92 % and 97 %. All the evaluation indexes of the SVR model for predicting 
the compressive strength of concrete are better than those of the BPNN model. Consequently, the 
SVR model can be utilized to accurately evaluate concrete compressive strength during the 
structural performance assessment of existing buildings.   
1. Introduction 
With the increasing development of high-rise buildings and infrastructure construction both domestically and internationally, the 
repair and renovation of existing building structures have become crucial research areas in current engineering construction [1]. 
Among various building materials, concrete, widely utilized in construction, relies on its compressive strength as a primary indicator of 
structural quality [2,3]. Generally, the compressive strength of concrete is evaluated mainly by destructive testing method [3–5]. 
However, destructive tests not only incur high costs but also compromise the performance of the existing structure, making them less 
feasible for deteriorating concrete structures [4,6]. Therefore, non-destructive testing (NDT) techniques have gained popularity for

assessing the quality of concrete structures, particularly in terms of compressive strength [5,7–9]. Compared to destructive testing, 
NDT reduces the testing workload, minimizes damage to the original structure, and decreases testing costs, while still providing 
reliable and accurate results [10]. To enhance the accuracy of predicted outcomes, many studies recommend employing combinations 
of NDT methods to estimate concrete compressive strength [11,12]. 
More specifically, non-destructive techniques were proposed by many scholars in the middle of the last century to evaluate the 
compressive strength of concrete. Among these techniques, the ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) and rebound hammer number (RN) 
tests are the most widely adopted for evaluating compressive strength [12,13]. Furthermore, NDT provides comprehensive infor­
mation on concrete properties and homogeneity assessment [14–16]. Consequently, various NDT techniques can be employed to 
evaluate concrete compressive strength. Previous studies have discussed the UPV and RN methods either independently or in com­
bination. Typically, combining UPV and RN measurements yields a more accurate estimation of concrete strength. Some literature 
proposes merging these two NDT methods to create a precise prediction model, while others utilize a single NDT method for strength 
prediction [12,17]. Some studies have concluded that the effectiveness of the combined method, compared to individual methods, 
depends on the number of specimens used in the calibration process [12,13]. 
The preliminary application of NDT in concrete is mainly based on the classification of concrete according to its mechanical 
properties. One of the most representative is the classification of concrete quality proposed by Whitehurst. He proposed the charac­
terization of concrete quality with ultrasonic velocity, which can be classified into five quality classes [18]. Other researchers also 
proposed to characterize concrete quality based on the RN values of the concrete under study. In 1949, Leslie and Cheesman [19] 
proposed a more specialized concrete classification using ultrasonic velocity measurements and RN values to classify concrete quality 
into four categories. 
In the 1960s, a large number of empirical equations for estimating the compressive strength of concrete based on UPV and RN 
values were proposed by many researchers and subjected to multiple regression analysis [20,21]. These equations were determined by 
regression analysis on a series of experimental datasets, including power functions, bilinear functions, and biexponential functions 
[22–24]. In addition, Lawson et al. [25] investigated the relationship between UPV and the compressive strength of concrete. The 
relationship between UPV and the compressive strength of concrete increases with age, but the rate of increase is dependent on the mix 
proportion designs and curing method. Sbartai et al. [26] proposed a method for obtaining UPV and compressive strength data for 
concrete using a comprehensive database of NDT experiments. They also conducted an evaluation of existing NDT techniques for 
assessing concrete compressive strength. Jain et al. [27] combined different NDTs to estimate the compressive strength of concrete. 
Ali-Benyahia et al. [2] proposed some models between destructive testing and individual or combined NDTs for a more accurate 
assessment of concrete strength. The results of the study showed that the combined method is of high value in concrete strength 
estimation compared to the existing models. Ali Poorarbabi et al. [1] carried out nondestructive testing of a large number of concrete 
specimens for UPV and RN. An effective Response Surface Method (RSM) was proposed for estimating the compressive strength of 
concrete, which is more accurate than other available models. Khuram Rashid et al. [8] tested the UPV, RN, and compressive strength 
of concrete at different curing ages, respectively. The relationship between UPV-compressive strength and RN-compressive strength 
was established. Their combination was used to predict compressive strength and the correctness of the predicted values was verified. 
These equations were primarily used to estimate the compressive strength of concrete using UPV values, RN values, or NDT combi­
nation measurements. However, there is a high bias in the estimation of these relationships. The estimated compressive strength values 
differed from the actual values directly measured through destructive laboratory testing. 
Traditional models focus on mathematical analysis methods, which mainly include statistical analysis based on linear or nonlinear 
regression equations. Due to the nonlinear relationship between concrete composition and compressive strength, it is difficult to obtain 
accurate regression equations, which requires a great deal of skill and experience. Machine learning techniques are important tools for 
the prediction of nonlinear relationships and have a wide range of applications in civil engineering [28–31]. Some of the machine 
learning techniques used to develop prediction models for complex problems include Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support 
Vector Machines (SVM), and Relevance Vector Machines (RVM) [32–34]. Among them, artificial neural networks are most widely used 
to solve the problem of prediction of concrete compressive strength by machine learning techniques [35–38]. Bilgehan and Turgut [39] 
proposed an ANN model based on the UPV results of specimens to predict concrete compressive strength. The developed model showed 
a high degree of accuracy and the model can be used to evaluate the compressive strength of concrete. Erda et al. [40] used artificial 
neural networks and multiple regression techniques to predict the compressive strength of vacuum-treated concrete. The performance 
of the proposed single and multiple regression models in predicting the compressive strength of concrete was compared. The per­
formance of the developed artificial neural network prediction model using NDT results was superior to the regression equations. Atici 
[41] also used multiple regression techniques and artificial neural network to predict the compressive strength of concrete. The results 
showed that the artificial neural network model had better prediction performance than multiple regression. Lorenzi’s study showed 
that artificial neural network prediction can obtain concrete compressive strength at a lower computational cost. Emad et al. [42,43] 
developed four different soft computing techniques to predict the compressive strength of ultra-high performance fiber concrete and 
found that the ANN model was more suitable than the others. Mahmood et al. [44] found that the compressive strength of cemented 
grouted sand can be well predicted using the ANN model. Alkayem et al.[45] generalized and summarized the research on predicting 
the performance of concrete at high temperatures using machine learning and deep learning. Skentou et al. [46] combined ultrasonic 
pulse velocity, Schmidt hammer rebound number and effective porosity NDT metrics, and ANN modeling to predict granite unconfined 
compressive strength. Asteris and Mokos [47] developed multiple back-propagation neural network models. They proposed three 
different models to predict the compressive strength, including a single input with only UPV values or RN values, and predicting the 
output using both UPV and RN value numbers. In all cases, satisfactory results were obtained during the training and testing phases. 
Asteris et al. [5] established several soft computing techniques to estimate concrete compressive strength using UPV and rebound

Since the existing methods of predicting concrete compressive strength using NDT and machine learning methods fail to account for 
the concrete mix proportion design and curing age, both of which can significantly impact concrete strength development [48]. To 
address this gap, data sets comprising 180 concrete strength specimens with varying mix proportion designs and curing ages were 
collected from the literature to establish an effective and reliable method for predicting compressive strength [3]. This study proposes a 
novel approach that combines concrete mix proportioning and NDT (specifically, UPV and RN) to predict compressive strength, 
leveraging machine learning computational techniques to improve the accuracy and reliability of predictions. Support vector machines 
and neural networks are widely used in engineering applications due to their proven predictive ability, making them ideal for this 
study [49,50]. Accordingly, SVR and BPNN are used to predict compressive strength based on NDT data, with consideration given to 
concrete mix proportion design and curing age. The resulting SVR and BPNN models are evaluated using various machine learning 
performance metrics to assess their effectiveness in predicting compressive strength. 
2. Research significance 
Concrete, as the most commonly used building material in construction and infrastructure development, plays a crucial role in 
determining the structural performance of buildings. Among various indicators, the assessment of compressive strength stands out as a 
key parameter for evaluating the quality and durability of concrete structures. Despite numerous research efforts focusing on NDT 
techniques to assess and forecast concrete compressive strength, there is a growing interest in integrating NDT methods with machine 
learning approaches to enhance prediction accuracy in construction engineering. Existing literature highlights a gap in the current 
methodologies for predicting concrete compressive strength, as they often overlook key factors such as concrete mix proportion design 
and curing age. These factors play a significant role in influencing concrete strength development, ultimately impacting the perfor­
mance of regression models employed for compressive strength prediction. 
In this study, we address this gap by collecting a comprehensive dataset comprising 180 concrete strength specimens with diverse 
proportion designs and curing ages sourced from literature. By integrating concrete mix proportion design with NDT test results, we 
aim to develop a robust and accurate predictive model for concrete compressive strength. Notably, our research introduces the novel 
approach of utilizing SVR and BPNN models to predict the compressive strength of concrete specimens, leveraging information from 
both concrete mix proportion design and NDT tests (UPV and RN). Furthermore, we conduct a thorough evaluation of the proposed 
models using various machine learning performance metrics to assess their efficacy in predicting concrete compressive strength. 
3. Source of dataset 
The first step in predicting the compressive strength of concrete using NDT and machine learning methods is to create a sample 
dataset. This section focuses on the NDT testing methods and dataset sources for the sample data. 
3.1. NDT methods 
The UPV testing is a widely used NDT method that measures the velocity of a compression stress wave (p-wave) in a material [12]. 
The principle of the UPV test is based on the propagation velocity of an ultrasonic pulse through the material. The equipment used for 
the test typically consists of a transmitter, a receiver, and a display device that shows the time taken for the pulse to travel through the 
material. The pulse velocity is commonly defined as the distance traveled by the pulse divided by the time taken [24].

The UPV method is widely employed in NDT for construction materials. It can be conducted either in a laboratory setting or at the 
construction site. This method is applicable for various purposes, including strength estimation, uniformity evaluation, durability 
analysis, surface crack depth measurement, and determination of the dynamic modulus of elasticity for materials such as concrete, 
rock, and wood [51–55]. 
The UPV method relies solely on the elastic properties of the material and is independent of its geometry. It is an effective approach 
for assessing the quality of concrete [56]. The principle of the UPV test is illustrated in Fig. 1. This test is used to evaluate the strength, 
uniformity, and quality of concrete. A higher pulse velocity indicates good quality and uniformity of the concrete, while a lower pulse 
velocity suggests the presence of non-uniformities such as cracks or voids. UPV test methods can be categorized as direct, semi-direct, 
or indirect tests. 
The rebound hammer test is a widely used NDT method for assessing the elastic properties and strength of concrete. It operates on 
the principle of rebound, where the energy absorbed by the concrete surface is related to the strength of the specimen [13,57]. Proper 
test preparation and selection of the specimen testing surface are crucial for obtaining accurate results. The test surface should be 
carefully prepared by smoothing it with an abrasive stone. A certain amount of energy is then applied to the surface by striking it with a 
hammer. The plunger of the hammer must be perpendicular to the smooth surface, and the rebound numbers are recorded, with at least 
10 records taken for each surface tested. 
Due to the challenge of establishing a direct correlation between the RN and the concrete strength, the rebound hammer test is most 
suitable for large areas with similar types of concrete. It is recommended to combine this test with other well-known non-destructive 
tests to obtain a comprehensive assessment of concrete strength. Numerous studies highlight the advantages of the rebound hammer 
test for concrete, while also emphasizing the need for complementary tests to accurately determine concrete strength.

4. Methodology 
To comprehensively analyze the relationship between UPV, RN, and concrete compressive strength, this study employs a machine 
learning approach to predict concrete compressive strength. This section focuses on the machine learning methodology used in the 
study and the evaluation indicators for the performance of the prediction model. The dataset S = {(xi ,yi), i=1,2,…l} is utilized, where 
xi represents the influencing factors of the ith training sample, including concrete mix proportion design, UPV, and RN, serving as the 
model input. Meanwhile, yi represents the corresponding concrete compressive strength, serving as the model output. The model 
sample data is divided into a training set and a test set in a 3:1 ratio. The flowchart depicting the model building process is displayed in  
Fig. 4. 
4.1. Support vector regression 
Support Vector Machine is a statistical learning theory proposed by Vapnik [58], which is based on the criterion of structural risk 
minimization [58,59]. This method aims to minimize both the structural risk and the sample error, thereby improving the general­
ization ability of the model without being limited by the dimensionality of the data. To address regression fitting problems using SVM, 
Vapnik et al. [58] introduced an insensitive loss function ε, resulting in a regression-type support vector machine. This model exhibits 
superior performance and effectiveness. In SVM regression fitting, the main idea is to determine an optimal plane that minimizes the 
error between the training samples and the optimal plane [60].

4.2. BP neural network 
Considering the advantages of neural networks in handling complex nonlinear relationships, this study incorporates neural 
network modeling into the prediction of concrete strength. The artificial neural network model is a mathematical tool that emulates 
the functions of the human brain, such as learning, reasoning, and performing parallel computations [5,49]. The back propagation (BP) 
neural network consists of an input layer, an intermediate layer (also known as the hidden layer), and an output layer, as shown in  
Fig. 6. In Fig. 6, xi represents the input vector X= (x1, x2,…xi…,xn), yi represents the output vector Y= (y1, y2, …yi…, yn), and Wij and Wjk 
represent the connection weights between the input layer and the hidden layer. When considering concrete mix proportion design, the 
architecture of BPNN is divided into 3 layers with the number of neurons in each layer being 7,10 and 1, respectively. 
The learning process of the BPNN consists of two stages [31,47]. In the first stage, information is propagated forward from the input 
layer through the hidden layer to the output layer. By inputting the sample data, the input vector is processed through the designed 
network structure and the weights and thresholds are adjusted iteratively to achieve sample learning. In the second stage, information 
is propagated backward from the output layer to the input layer, following a specific rule to modify the initial weights and thresholds of 
the algorithm. This process aims to facilitate the convergence of the algorithm. The first stage of the learning process is completed 
when information flows from the input layer to the output layer. If there is a discrepancy between the output result of the output layer 
and the expected result, the error is propagated backward from the output layer to the input layer through the intermediate layer. The 
weights are adjusted layer by layer using the gradient descent algorithm to complete the second stage of the learning process. These 
two stages are repeated alternately until convergence is achieved, signaling the end of the learning process and the termination of 
network training.

6. Discussion 
In Europe, America, Japan, and other developed countries, the construction of high-rise buildings and infrastructure has undergone 
large-scale rapid development, which has now transitioned to the stage of maintenance and repair of existing structures. The repair and 
renovation of existing building structures have emerged as a prominent research area in current engineering construction. The sub­
stantial economic burden brought on by the deterioration of concrete structures has far exceeded initial expectations, prompting 
widespread attention to the durability of concrete structures as a primary indicator of structural quality. NDT techniques are widely 
employed to evaluate the quality of concrete structures, particularly in terms of concrete compressive strength. 
Existing studies have revealed that concrete mix design and curing age are often overlooked in predicting concrete compressive 
strength using NDT and machine learning methods, even though they significantly impact the strength prediction. Therefore, this 
study aims to introduce an effective approach for predicting concrete compressive strength by integrating concrete mix design and 
NDT test results. The study establishes a SVR model for concrete compressive strength and a BPNN prediction model based on NDT test 
outcomes, considering the influence of concrete mix design and curing age. Utilizing the established concrete mix design scheme, a 
dataset S={(xi, yi)，i=1,2,…,l} comprising concrete UPV, RN, and compressive strength data is employed. Concrete compressive

the SVR model and the BPNN model exhibited improved fitting of the training set when considering concrete mix proportion design.

SVR model significantly outperforming the BPNN model. 
Comparison with the relevant literature, specifically the work of Ali Poorarbabi and Mohammadreza Ghasemi et al. [1], who aimed 
to estimate the compressive strength of concrete using the response surface method by testing the UPV and RN of concrete specimens, 
showed that the R2 of their prediction model for concrete compressive strength at 7, 28, and 90 days were 86.3 %, 87.1 %, and 93.4 % 
respectively, with an overall R2 of 86.8 %. However, their proposed concrete compressive strength prediction model exhibited 
significantly lower accuracy compared to the SVR and BPNN prediction models proposed in our study. Asteris et al. [5] developed six 
soft computing models to predict concrete compressive strength using ultrasonic pulse velocity and rebound hammer tests. The most 
accurate prediction of concrete compressive strength was obtained for BPNN. The corresponding coefficient of determination R2 was 
95.03 %. It was slightly lower than the coefficient of determination of 98.3 % for the SVR prediction model used in this study. 
The results depict the high prediction accuracy of the concrete compressive strength prediction model established in this study, 
which leverages NDT of concrete and machine learning methods, and its potential for generalization. However, the limited size of the 
sample dataset for concrete mix proportion design, UPV, RN, and compressive strength is a notable limitation. Future research could 
address this by collecting a larger sample size. Additionally, with the rapid development of group intelligence methods, novel heuristic 
algorithms such as the Whale Optimization Algorithm and Gray Wolf Optimization Algorithm could be utilized to optimize the SVR 
model parameters in upcoming studies.
