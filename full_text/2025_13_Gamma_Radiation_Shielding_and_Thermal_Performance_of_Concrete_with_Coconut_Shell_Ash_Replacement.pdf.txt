Abstract 
Keywords Radiation Shielding; GRSA; 
CSA; Weight Percent; DTA; TGA. 
 
This research assessed the influence of replacing cement with 
coconut shell ash (CSA) on radiation shielding ability of a 
concrete using Nist XCOM, Phy-X, and Geant4. Four concrete 
C1, C2, C3, and C4 were prepared with water-to-cement (W/C) 
ratio of 0.6 throughout the mixes. The densities of the resulting 
concretes were altered (2.40 g/cm3 to 2.20 g/cm3) as the CSA 
content raises from 0.00 kg to 0.15 kg. More so, adding CSA to 
the concrete matrix, resulted in variation of linear attenuation 
coefficients (LAC) (0.195 cm-1 for C1, 0.18 cm-1 for C2, 0.183 
cm-1 for C3, and 0.177 cm-1 for C4) of the resulting composite at 
0.662 MeV. Based on this study, it was observed that samples C4 
reduces the incoming photons better than C2, C3, and C1 at 
lower energy level (0.015 MeV to 0.04 MeV), while samples C1 
reduces the incoming photons better than C2, C3, and C4 at 
higher energy level (0.04 MeV to 15 MeV). The thermal 
performance of the resulting concrete C2, C3, and C1 were 
improved when compared with sample C4. Therefore, concrete 
samples C2, C3, and C1 is the most effective for radiation 
shielding for their resistance to thermal threats.

Introduction 
Nuclear innovation is considered as the alternate source of energy which when utilized could mitigate the unexpected 
power issues [1]. This energy coming from nuclear technology gives out ionizing rays which could be very dangerous 
to human health [2]. It is for this reason that shielding becomes necessary in order to minimize the level of these rays

coming from their source to the nearest minimal, so that the population leaving close to the facilities that emits such 
hazardous radiation can be protected from the dangers of the rays [3, 4]. The massless and chargeless nature of gamma 
rays made them highly penetrative, necessitating the need to search for high proton number (Z) and density materials 
in order to achieve a better shielding against the harmful radiation [5]. 
On that note, researchers suggested ceramics, lead, metal alloys and Clay for gamma radiation shielding because of their 
high Z, high densities [6, 7]. But the limitations like frigidity for ceramics, low mechanical strength for Clay, health 
risks for metal alloys, and high cost as well as toxicity for lead, calls for a serious concern [8]. Concretes has been the 
substitute for these materials because of their high density and easy to operate, which makes them good for gamma 
radiation shielding [9]. On the quest to improve the shielding ability of concretes, researchers suggested that, concrete 
should be mixed with local wastes since some of these local wastes are high Z materials. Therefore, when local wastes 
like coconut shell powder is mixed with concrete, because of the silicon in it, the shielding ability of the resulting 
composite is expected to be altered, and also, because of the calcium content in it, the thermal resistance of the resulting 
concrete could be improved [10]. 
Therefore, it is vital to explore the addition of local content into concrete, and also to check the parameters like 
differential thermal analysis (DTA), which gives the extent of the material to withstand high degree of temperature 
generated by electromagnetic radiation before getting deformed [11-13]. 
Thermal performance of a material to be utilized for gamma radiation shielding is also very important to be assessed, 
as it gives the clear insight the amount of temperature (generated from electromagnetic radiation) that a material can 
withstand before getting deformed or cracked. It was reported by [14] that, some of these local materials like coconut 
shell ash (CSA), rice husk ash (RHA), bone ash (BA), palm nut shell ash (PNSA), and the rest, can improve the thermal 
resistance of a material due to the presence of calcium in them [15]. It is also crucial to check the linear attenuation 
coefficient (LAC, cm-1) which describes the easiness of a material to shield radiation [16, 17]. 
By repurposing coconut shells, a typically discarded waste material, the utilization of CSA is viewed as a sustainable 
practice that can lead to a reduction in concrete production's environmental impact [18]. Moreover, since it is relatively 
cost-effective in comparison to alternative additives characterized by high density, CSA is a viable solution for 
increasing concrete's ability to shield against radiation from an economic perspective [19]. 
In this study, chemical content of the concrete-CSA is checked by the Energy Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF), 
and the data obtained are used to compute the GRSA for the resulting concrete-CSA material with the aid of Phy-
X/PSD, Nist XCOM, and Grant4 software. The thermal characterization of the fabricated materials is also conducted in 
the work using DTA which depict the degree of temperature that a material can withstand before getting deformed or 
cracked. 
2.0. Methodology 
2.1. Production of Concrete - Coconut Shell Ash (CSA) Materials  
Figure 1 depicted how concrete samples were prepared. C1, containing 0 wt% of CSA content replacing cement, was 
prepared by blending 0.5 kg of cement, 1 kg of sand, and 1 kg of granite. C2, with 10 wt% of CSA content as a substitute 
of cement, was prepared as follows: 0.45 kg of cement, 1 kg of sand, 1 kg of granite, and 0.05 kg of CSA. C3, having 
20 wt% of the content of CSA as replacement of cement, was achieved by the mixture of 0.4 kg of cement, 1 kg of sand, 
1 kg of granite, and 0.1 kg of CSA. C4, which has 30 wt% of CSA content replacing cement in it, was prepared with 
0.35 kg of cement, 1 kg of sand, 1 kg of granite, and 0.15 kg of CSA. In this research work, we adopted 0.6 as the 
fraction of water-cement (W/C) for all mixtures [20, 21]. The resulting mixture was shaped like discs after a rigorous 
stirring, later which the molded concretes were spread under the sun for some days to regularize their weight. In order 
to make sure that the samples were free from the water content, the samples were put into an oven and the temperature 
of the oven was set to low degree for two hours.

2.2. Sample Analysis 
The specimen's elemental composition was characterized through the analytical technique of energy-dispersive X-ray 
(EDX) spectroscopy, whereby a sample was bombarded by a targeted electron beam which displaced electrons from 
their atomic orbits, while distinctive X-rays are emitted. The bombardment of the sample with electrons causes ejection 
of electrons, with vacancies being created and populated by electrons of a lower energy, while as they relax excess 
energy in the X-ray form is subsequently released. During the period of relaxation, the energy given is absolutely distinct 
for each and every of the element within present withing the sample. In essence, each and every element possesses their 
own completely unique X-ray signature, as such, the X-rays emitted from the specimen furnished qualitative 
compositional insights [14, 24]. This qualitative compositions of the elements for the produced samples in wt% were 
carried out using ARL QUANT'X EDXRF Analyzer at Umaru Musa Yar’adua University (UMYU), Katsina, Nigeria, 
and the results are presented in Table 2. 
The research conducted DTA test to measure the thermal transition of each of the samples through observation of 
temperature difference between the control material (Al2O3) and produced samples. Same quantity of both control and 
produced samples were respectively placed on the control and sample pans, and they were both exposed to same quantity 
of heat between room-temperature up to 200 0C. The graph of graph and sample temperature different was plotted as a 
function of temperature, and the endothermic and exothermic transition in the samples was obtained.

Figure 2: Linear attenuation coefficient (LAC) of the Fabricated Concretes against Energy 
A fast drop in the values of LAC (22.049 cm-1 to 7.358 cm-1 for C1, 21.098 cm-1 to 6.965 cm-1 for C2, 19.105 cm-1 to 
6.302 cm-1 for C3, and 24.008 cm-1 to 7.899 cm-1 for C4) was observed between the lower energy range of 0.02 MeV 
to 0.03 MeV. Between the energy range of 0.03 MeV to 15 MeV, a smooth decrease in the values of LAC was observed 
from 7.358 cm-1 to 0.056 cm-1 for C1, 6.965 cm-1 to 0.053 cm-1 for C2, 6.302 cm-1 to 0.053 cm-1 for C3, and 7.899 cm-
1 to 0.053 cm-1 for C4. The overall reduction in the values of the LAC of the studied samples could be attributed to the 
decrease in the number of interactions at higher energy, confirming that, the shielding ability of the fabricated concretes 
is less at higher energy. On the other hand, at lower energy level, say 0.015 MeV, the values of the LAC of the studied 
concretes sample tend to slightly increase (25.555 cm-1, for C1, 28.956 cm-1 for C2, 28.067 cm-1 for C3, and 30.482 cm-
1 for C4) as the content of CSA is added to the concrete matrix. This increased in the values of LAC of concrete C4, 
C3, and C2 compared to C1 could be attributed to the increase in the concentration by Wt.% of silicon, Sulphur, calcium, 
potassium, and strontium in concrete C4, C3, and C2 compared to C1, confirming that, at lower energy, concrete C4 
has the better gamma radiation shielding ability. Contrarily, at higher energy (5 MeV), the LAC of the studied concretes 
samples tend to slightly decrease as the content of CSA is added to the concrete matrix. This decrease for example 0.071 
cm-1 for C1, 0.066 cm-1 for C2, 0.067 cm-1 for C3, and 0.065 cm-1 for C4 could be related to the high penetration of 
gamma radiation at higher energy, which resulted in a smaller number of interactions, confirming that, at higher energy 
level, concrete C1 has the better gamma radiation shielding ability. This result is in line with the report of [8]. 
The LAC at lower energy between 0.015 MeV to 0.04 MeV, raises as the content of CSA was slowly added to the 
concrete matrix. Sample C4 which has 150 g of CSA replacing cement consistently exhibited the highest LAC values 
at lower energies, making 150 g of CSA replacement a threshold for optimum concrete mix in radiation shielding 
application. The raise in LAC within this energy interval could be connected to the continual increase in the high atomic 
number (Z) elements like Strontium (Sr), Calcium (Ca), and Silicon (Si) concentrations with increase in the CSA content 
from 0 g to 150 g across the concrete samples as presented in Table 2. Their high Z could be responsible for the raise 
in the overall effective atomic number (Zeff) of the concretes, resulting in an increase in density, being the major factor 
responsible for the improved LAC of our samples [15]. The superior LAC experienced at these energy intervals could 
also be related to the fact that in the lower energy region, the possibility of interactions is high, which could result in 
high absorption of the incoming photon radiation [14]. On the contrary, at higher energy, the LAC was observed to have 
decreased with raise in the content of CSA across the concrete matrix. This reduction in the LAC values could be 
attributed to the fact that, the probability of interaction reduces as the energy increase [25]. 
The derived weight loss curve is generally more sensitive to subtle differences in weight loss than the DTA curve and 
is therefore most commonly used for comparing similar materials. Figure 3 compares the curves derived for samples 
C1, C2, C3 and C4 by traditional DTA (steady rate of heating), where a minimum variation was observed. The weight 
loss in C2 was observed to be the least according to the figure, with sample C4 having the greatest loss of weight, which 
could be attributed to the calcium carbonate decomposition and the CO2 gas evaporation. The temperatures obtained are 
similar to those presented by [2, 26]. Each of such temperature of dehydroxylation is sufficient enough to offer each 
sample an ability of withstanding the possible internal heat which could be generated from gamma rays. Based on the 
results, sample C2 could withstand the heat generated by gamma radiation better than sample C3, then sample C1, and 
sample C4. This result is in line with the report of [2, 14].

4.0. Conclusion 
At lower energy range (0.015 MeV to 0.04 MeV), it was observed that the composite samples C4 is better attenuating 
than concrete C2, C3, and C1 in shielding gamma rays, making 150 g of CSA replacement a threshold for optimum 
concrete mix in radiation shielding application at lower energy range between 0.015 MeV to 0.04 MeV. While at 
medium and higher energy range (0.06 MeV to 0.15 MeV), it was observed that the composite samples C1 is better 
attenuating than concrete C2, C3, and C4, making 0 g of CSA replacement a threshold for optimum concrete mix in 
radiation shielding application at higher energy interval of 0.06 MeV to 0.15 MeV. The introduction of CSA into the 
concrete matrix increases the thermal performance of the concrete as they were to withstand high degree heat generated 
by electromagnetic (EM) radiation before getting deformed, confirming that, the samples with CSA content can be 
applied in shielding of gamma radiation. Based on the findings of this study, the radiation subjections by staff during 
procedures in the nuclear medicine rooms could be greatly minimize by application of concretes mixed with CSA as 
shielding material. The application of concrete incorporated with CSA in shielding in a non-destructive testing (NDT) 
laboratory, safety could be assured, since the radiation coming from inspection equipment can be blocked, particularly 
at lower energies. Our materials could be utilized in the nuclear power plants as structural compositions in order to 
safeguard the radiation workers as well as the environment from ionizing effects of radiation, donating greatly to the 
operational safety and management of wastes. The high thermal resistance of our samples could be a great advantage 
to building industries, especially where high temperature structural buildings are required.
