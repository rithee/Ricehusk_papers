Abstract. The increasing emphasis on sustainable infrastructure has prompted the utilization of alternative 
and eco-friendly materials in pavement construction. This study investigates the potential use of rice husk 
ash (RHA) and Bagasse ash (BA), both agricultural waste by-products, as alternative fillers in Asphalt 
Concrete–Binder Course (AC-BC) mixtures. A quantitative experimental approach was used, involving 
laboratory tests on aggregates, asphalt, and fillers, followed by Marshall testing. Filler contents of 0%, 2%, 
4%, 6%, and 8% were evaluated for stability, flow, void in mix (VIM), voids in mineral aggregate (VMA), 
voids filled with bitumen (VFB), and Marshall Quotient (MQ). The optimum asphalt content was 6.65%. 
Results showed that BA improved the mixtures mechanical properties, especially at 4% filler, achieves the 
highest stability and MQ. RHA performed well at lower levels, but excessive use increased stiffness and 
risk of cracking. Both fillers meet Indonesian National Standard (SNI) specifications, proving their technical 
feasibility. Furthermore, their application contributes to waste reduction and environmental sustainability 
by recycling biomass waste into valuable construction materials. This research confirms that RHA and BA 
are promising eco-friendly alternatives to conventional mineral fillers, aligning with sustainable 
infrastructure goals without compromising the structural integrity of pavement layers. 
1 Introduction 
Road pavements in tropical climates are vulnerable to 
early failure because of high surface temperatures, 
frequent heavy rainfall, and substantial traffic loading.  
Previous studies have shown that elevated temperatures 
combined with heavy loads to intensify rutting, while 
moisture and rainfall accelerate the decline in pavement 
performance and service life [1]. 
Asphalt mixtures require reinforcement through 
additives mixed into the asphalt to modify its properties, 
preventing brittleness at low temperatures and reducing 
the risk of cracking [2]. Moreover, modification also aims 
to introduce new characteristics to asphalt and to improve 
its stability and strength. 
In road pavement mixtures, the materials used 
typically consist of asphalt, fine aggregates, coarse 
aggregates, and filler. Fillers are materials used to fill the 
voids between fine and coarse aggregates and can be 
derived from natural or manufactured crushed rock. 
Examples of fillers that can be used include agricultural 
by products including rice husk ash and bagasse ash [3, 
4]. 
The transition toward sustainable infrastructure has 
become a global concern, including in Indonesia, 
especially as flexible pavements are considered more 
efficient in their application. The commonly used fillers

Indonesia's large agricultural sector produces a 
significant amount of waste. Therefore, this research 
seeks to utilize agricultural waste that was previously 
considered useless into something valuable. RHA and BA 
both agricultural waste products, are implemented as filler 
substances in asphalt pavement formulations in this 
examination. The primary objective involves assessing 
the influence of utilizing these agricultural residues as 
filler components. This application of agricultural waste-
based fillers is an effort to support environmentally 
friendly filler material utilization [14].

2.2 Mix design 
The mixture formulation was established through the 
Marshall method protocols to identify the Optimum 
Asphalt Content (OAC). Upon determining the OAC, 
Marshall testing was conducted by integrating rice husk 
ash and bagasse ash as filler additives into the composite 
matrix. Assessment criteria encompassed stability, flow, 
voids filled with bitumen, Void In mix     and mineral 
aggregate. Aggregate gradation adhesion to asphalt 
concrete Binder Course requirement specified in Bina 
Marga specification (2018). 
Aggregate gradation adhered to asphalt specified is 
carried out using a series of Marshall method procedures 
to determine the Optimum Asphalt Content (OAC). After 
obtaining the OAC, Marshall tests are performed by 
adding rice husk ash and sugarcane bagasse ash fillers to 
the material composition. The parameters analyzed 
include Stability, Flow, Voids in Mix (VIM), Voids Filled 
with Bitumen (VFB), and Voids in Mineral Aggregate 
(VMA). The aggregate proportions are determined 
according to the Asphalt Concrete - Binder Course (AC-
BC) gradation based on the Bina Marga Specification 
(2018 Revision 2).

2.3.3 Marshall testing 
Marshall testing is performed after obtaining the 
Theoretical Asphalt Content (TAC). The values obtained 
from the Marshall test are recorded in the Marshall table 
to determine the Optimum Asphalt Content (OAC). The 
OAC is determined by varying the asphalt content, 
plotting the results in typical graphs, identifying the 
asphalt content that meets the specifications, and 
calculating the average value from the most frequently 
compliant data across each typical graph. The standard 
Marshall test uses 15 specimens with 5 different asphalt 
content levels. For the durability Marshall test, 12 
specimens are used with 4 different soaking durations. 
Immersion in a 60 ˚C water bath for 30 minutes consistent 
with AASHTO T245 standard, simulating typical tropical 
field conditions and evaluating the mix’s resistance to 
thermal degradation.

3.3.6 Marshall quotient 
Rice Husk Ash (RHA) significantly increases the 
stiffness of the mixture at 4% content, but this can lead to 
cracking due to excessive rigidity. In contrast, Bagasse 
Ash (BA) provides more stable MQ values, resulting in a 
well-balanced mixture with both strength and flexibility, 
making it more resistant to deformation without being 
prone to cracking. 
 
Fig.  9. MQ vs filler content 
Thus, as shown in Fig. 9, RHA values meet the 
specification. The highest MQ is at 4% (1228.70 Kg/mm), 
indicating high stiffness, but excessive stiffness can 
reduce flexibility. BA all values meet the specification, 
showing an optimal balance between strength and 
deformation resistance. 
The trends observed in stability and flow data suggest 
that both types of ash exhibit optimal performance at 
moderate filler content (4-6%) with diminishing results 
beyond this range. This can be attributed to excessive 
filler interfering with the optimal bitumen aggregate 
matrix, potentially leading to increased stiffness and 
brittleness. The higher Marshall Quotient (MQ) observed 
in BA mixtures indicates better load-bearing capacity and 
resistance to deformation, likely due to the angular and 
porous structure of BA particles that enhances interlock 
and binder absorption.  
3.4 Comparative analysis 
The comparative analysis of the Marshall test results 
demonstrates clear differences in performance between 
Rice Husk Ash (RHA) and sugarcane Bagasse Ash (BA) 
as fillers in AC-BC mixtures. In terms of stability, RHA 
exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing filler content, 
indicating reduced load-bearing capacity due to increased 
mixture stiffness. In contrast, BA significantly improved 
stability, with the highest value observed at 4% filler 
content, suggesting its superior ability to enhance 
structural strength. Regarding flow, RHA resulted in 
values below the standard range, indicating a stiff and 
potentially brittle mix. At the same time, BA produced 
flow values above the acceptable range, suggesting 
excessive softness and a risk of deformation under traffic 
loads. 
For voids in mix (VIM), RHA caused higher porosity 
at greater filler contents, whereas BA maintained VIM 
values within the ideal range at 4–8% filler levels. Both 
fillers yielded acceptable voids in mineral aggregate 
(VMA) values, though BA showed slightly more 
consistent results. In terms of voids filled with bitumen 
(VFB), RHA provided more balanced values across filler 
variations, while BA tended to retain excessive asphalt at 
low 
contents, 
risking 
bleeding. 
However, 
BA 
demonstrated more stable Marshall Quotient (MQ) 
values, reflecting a better balance between strength and 
flexibility compared to RHA, which showed excessively 
high stiffness at 4% filler content. 
3.5 Limitations and future work 
Based on the overall findings, sugarcane bagasse ash 
(BA) is identified as the more effective filler material in 
AC-BC mixtures. Its ability to significantly increase 
mixture 
stability, 
maintain 
balanced 
mechanical 
characteristics, and provide consistent performance across 
several Marshall parameters makes it more suitable than 
RHA. Additionally, as an agricultural waste product, BA 
represents a sustainable and environmentally friendly 
alternative to conventional fillers. The optimum 
performance of BA was achieved at a 4% filler content, 
making it a highly recommended choice for enhancing the 
durability and strength of asphalt concrete wearing 
courses.  
Higher stability and MQ values in BA mixtures could 
be attributed to better filler bitumen adhesion, supported 
by previous SEM observation on BA morphology, 
indicating porous and angular particles that enhance 
interlocking. 
Although this study demonstrates that both RHA and 
BA can serve as alternative fillers, their performance is 
influenced by their distinct chemical composition. The 
superior performance of BA and RHA is likely attributed 
to its higher pozzolanic activity and better particle 
morphology. RHA, rich in silica, may lead to excessive 
stiffness and brittleness at higher contents due to limited 
calcium oxide or other binding components. Future 
research should include a detailed chemical and 
microstructural analysis to better explain the mechanism 
behind the differences in performance. This difference in 
performance is attributed to the higher reactivity and 
pozzolanic index of Bagasse Ash due to its higher CaO 
and amorphous silica content. Whereas RHA, despite its 
high SiO2 content, has a denser structure and lower 
pozzolanic activity.

1. 
Stability increased significantly with BA, peaking at 
3453,42 Kg at 4% filler content, indicating enhanced 
load-bearing capacity. In contrast, RHA showed the 
highest stability (1795.48 kg) at 0% filler and 
decreased as filler content increased, suggesting a 
reduction in load capacity at higher dosages. 
2. 
Flow values revealed that excessive RHA content 
(≥4%) reduced flexibility to 0.91 mm, causing 
brittleness, while BA produced higher than standard 
values (5,03-6,20 mm), indicating greater flexibility 
but potential permanent deformation. 
3. 
Void in Mix (VIM) for RHA exceeded the ideal 3-
5% range at higher filler contents, whereas BA 
maintained VIM within acceptable limits at 4-8%, 
though too low (0.88%) at 2% filler. 
4. 
Void in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) remained within 
standards for both fillers, ensuring adequate binder 
coverage, with RHA generally giving slightly higher 
values. Exceeded 84% at lower contents, increasing 
bleeding risk 
5. 
Void-Filled with Bitumen (VFB) were more 
balanced in RHA mixtures, while BA achieved its 
peak (525.52 kg/mm) at 0% filler but dropped 
sharply with higher dosages. 
Overall, BA at 4% filler content is most effective for 
improving structural performance, offering higher 
stability and balanced flexibility, while RHA is better 
applied at lower contents to avoid excessive stiffness. 
Both materials meet Indonesia National Standards and 
support sustainable pavement construction by recycling 
agricultural waste into high-value fillers. 
Future work should explore long-term durability and 
binder-filler 
interaction 
mechanisms 
through 
microstructural analysis to optimize their application in 
flexible pavement.
