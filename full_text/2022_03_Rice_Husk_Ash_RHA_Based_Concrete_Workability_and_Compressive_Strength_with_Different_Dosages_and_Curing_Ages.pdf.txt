ABSTRACT: 
 
To reduce the consumption of cement in construction industry has been a hot area of consideration now days due to high evolution 
of CO2 gases during its production. Since decades efforts are made to replace cement with cementitious materials; minerals, 
agricultural, industrial by products etc. A few are fly ash, ground granulated blast furnace slag, silica fume, rice husk ash etc. These 
cementitious materials are often adopted as partial replacement of cement. However, their effectiveness and suitability as 
cementitious material depends upon their properties and the source of the production. Pakistan is an agricultural country and one of 
the major crops is Rice crop. It produces a substantial amount of rice husk during the processing g of the rice. A substantial amount 
of Rice Husk Ash (RHA) is produced on the burning of rice husk used as fuel in the rice mills while generating steam for parboiling 
process of rice grains. This ash causes the environmental problems also if not dumped properly. RHA is believed to have siliceous 
properties which may be used as supplementary cementitious material in concrete. As the suitability of supplementary cementitious 
materials is dependent upon the source of rice husk, temperature of burning of husk, its fineness and other properties. This study is 
focused on to determine the effect of rice husk ash produced by burning of the rice husk obtained from the local rice mills. 
Experimental investigation carried includes the determination of workability and compressive strength of concrete with different 
dosage of RHA from 5% to 30% with an increment of 5% tested at 1day, 3days, 7days and 28 days of the age of concrete. The 
results are compared with corresponding concrete without RHA and tested at the same ages. The results show the reduction in both 
the parameter; workability and compressive strength due to RHA particularly at its higher dosage beyond 10% by weight of cement.

1. INTRODUCTION 
Because of its structural stability and strength, the concrete is a 
popular building material used in the structures. Concrete is a 
mixture of cement, aggregates, and water. It plays a vital role in 
the economic development of any country. It is a durable 
material and possesses an unquestionable quality of life. The 
use of concrete in the construction industry has been essential 
due to its unmatchable performance compared to other 
materials. The manufacture of concrete in terms is continually 
growing manifold than the other materials being used for the 
construction. This is primarily due to human migration from 
rural to urban areas. However, this causes the massive 
consumption of the common ingredients normally obtained 
from natural resources. On the other hand, huge consumption of 
OPC results in greenhouse gas emissions during the cement 
manufacture, adversely affecting the environment. Altogether, 
the advancement in industrialization and agriculture has 
provoked another issue that also affects the environment. The 
problem is the generation of waste as industrial by-products, 
agricultural wastes etc. The dumping of this waste needs a vast 
area and if not appropriately dumped results in a hazardous 
effect on the environment, thereby badly affecting human 
health. For decades, efforts are being made by the construction 
industry and the researchers to find the ways and means to 
reduce the consumption of OPC in concrete and use these 
wastes as useful construction ingredients in concrete in general 
and as a binder in particular.  
 
Hence the researchers suggest the use of green materials which 
contain pozzolanic properties. Several green resources, such as 
fly ash, rice husk ash, blast furnace slag, silica fume, and 
damaged building materials have been investigated for partial 
substitution of cement. Recent technical advancements have 
demonstrated that these materials are useful materials that can 
create various valuable products. The cement and concrete 
industry has increasingly used mineral admixtures since the 
mid-twentieth century. Partial cement replacement addresses the 
growing demand for cement and concrete. This research focuses 
on studying the possibility of the partial replacement of cement 
with Rice Husk Ash (RHA). Rice husks are common 
agricultural byproducts. These are the shells generated during

2. LITERATURE REVIEW 
2.1 Rice Husk Ash 
The outer coating of the rice grain that is removed in the milling 
procedure is the rice husk. It is sometimes referred to as rice 
hull also and is a by product of rice milling. Rice husks account 
for about one-fifth of the world's 600 million tonnes of rice 
produced each year. From milled paddy, nearly 78% of the 
production is received in rice, broken grains, and bran while the 
remaining 22% of rice's weight is acquired as a husk. Rice husk 
contains a significant amount of Silicon. Hence it is classified as 
a pozzolanic substance. Pozzolanic materials are those that 
behave as siliceous and aluminous compounds. At room 
temperature, RHA will chemically react with calcium hydroxide 
during the hydration stage of Portland cement and when 
exposed to moisture (Boontawee et. al. 2018; Balapour et. al. 
2018). This husk is used for producing steam during the 
parboiling process through burning. It is reported that nearly 
22kg husk is produced when 1000kg of rice are processed. 
Pakistan produces 5.5million tones of rice resulting in 1.1 
million tonnes of husk production. The Rice Husk Ash (RHA) 
is a powder-formed material that is obtained by burning of rice 
husk. RHA is a potential pozzolanic substance that may be 
blended with cement to obtain durable concrete.

Adding the RHA in cement improves the early strength of the 
concrete and Calcium Silicate Hydrate (CSH) gel surrounding 
the cement particles. This gel is dense and less porous, 
potentially increasing the strength of the concrete against 
cracking. There have recently been significant attempts globally 
to use local and waste materials in concrete, such as rice husk. 
If rice husk is properly burned and suitably pulverized, it 
produces ash that may be utilised as a cement substitute.

2.3 RHA application in concrete 
RHA in recent years has gained attention by the researchers for 
using as supplementary cementitious material due to the high 
amount of amorphous silica content available in RHA. Various 
researchers have conducted the experimental work to assess the 
adaptability of RHA as a partial replacement of cement and 
reported that RHA could be show an increase in the strength of 
concrete up to 30% of cement replacement (Ganesan et. al. 
2008; Zareei et. al. 2017). However, the use of traditional RHA 
directly is unfavourable because the traditional RHA has shown 
variability in chemical and mineralogical characteristics, which 
leads to the need of testing characteristic of RHA before using 
in concrete. 
 
The application of RHA has resulted in several benefits, such as 
workability. RHA effects on the rheological characteristics, 
strength, finish, porosity, durability and cost of the concrete 
(Mehta and Monteiro, 2017). Research work shows that the use 
of RHA in concrete enhances the workability of fresh concrete 
and the strength and durability of hardened concrete, in addition 
to leading to sustainable concrete and improving the durability 
of hardened concrete (Bapat, 2012; Shetty, 2005). RHA's 
increased water needs have typically been attributed to their 
particle size and surface area. On the other hand, Givi et al. 
(2010a) and Givi et al. (2010b) discovered that the application 
of RHA decreases the water requirement of mixes. The 
workability of cement partially replaced by RHA is enhanced, 
amplified with coarser RHA particles (average particle size of 
95m) and raises the replacement ratio (Bui, 2001).  
 
In contrast, some researchers argued that increasing RHA 
quantity reduces the concrete workability due to absorptive 
property (Kartini, 2008). Also, the fineness of the particles 
causes’ high demand of water to the wet area of RHA, reducing 
workability (Ganesan et. al. 2008; Habeeb and Fayyadh, 2009). 
Besides this, the irregularity of the form of RHA particles also 
has a significant influence on the workability of the concrete 
(Srinivasreddy et. al. 2017). In assessing the performance of the 
concrete, one of the most critical parameters is the strength of 
the concrete. Strength is the material's capacity to withstand 
stress without failing (Neville and Brooks, 1987). In general, 
the concrete’s compressive strength depends on w/c ratio, 
mixture composition. The rate of strength growth at early ages 
is proportional to the rate of cement hydration. Portland cement 
contributes significantly to strong growth in the early stages, up 
to around seven days, after which it decreases significantly. As 
a result, the addition of RHA to concrete influences all aspects 
of its characteristics. RHA reacts as a cementitious material in 
the concrete.  
 
RHA is a strong pozzolanic material and this pozzolanic activity 
occurs due to silica content. An investigation of the effect of 
RHA and OPC-based concrete demonstrated that by replacing 
30% of the weight of OPC with RHA for 28 days, it is feasible 
to lower the temperature (Mehta and Pirtz, 1978). Another 
study investigated temperature range required to burn rice husk 
for obtaining the appropriate pozzolanic result where it was 
found that if the rice husk is burned at the optimal temperature, 
it is possible to substitute up to 40% of the cement with RHA 
with no noticeable difference in compressive strength compared 
to the controlled mix (Al-Khalaf and Yousif, 1984). Ryan 
(1999) performed an experimental investigation on the 
durability of concrete by studying the effect of sulphate attack 
and chloride ion penetration. Skalny et. al. (2002) argued that 
concrete subjected to sulphate assault experiences a progressive 
and significant reorganisation of its internal microstructure. 
These modifications have a direct impact on the material's 
technical 
characteristics. 
Oyetola 
and 
Adullahi 
(2006) 
calculated compressive strength of sand-crete block prepared by 
replacing cement with RHA up to 80% and reported increase in 
compressive strength of the ‘OPC/RHA sand-crete’ specimens 
with the age and decrease with the increase in RHA amount and 
for a specific mix, it is reached an ideal replacement level of 20 
per cent. The need for water is also high as the amount of rice 
husk rises. Prasad et al. (2006) researched cement concrete and 
found that cement mixed with sodium sulphate settings are 
superior to magnesium sulphate. The environment of 
magnesium sulphate is harder than the environment of sodium 
sulphate. Use of the RHA in substitution of cement showed that 
Rice Husk Ash's ideal substitution of cement was almost 10% 
working and resistance (Krishna et al. 2016). 
 
Study of hydration process and the development of compressive 
strength of concrete using RHA showed that the RHA particle 
size affected compressive strength for 28 days but replacement 
content took over for 91 days while the addition of hydration 
kinetics, the porous structure of RHA particles has enabled its 
function which enhances compression at a late age (Vieira et. al. 
2020). The compressive strength of the concrete using RHA as 
substitute for magnesium sulphate solution by replacing cement 
with rice husk ash at the rate of 0%, 5%, 5%, 10, 15%, and 20% 
by the mass of cement for the mixture. The findings revealed 
that with the addition of magnesium sulphate, RHA improves 
the concrete's durability (Sarma et al., 2014). Another study 
showed that concrete with use of RHA for replacing ordinary 
Portland cement can increase the compressive strength of the 
concrete up to the dosage of 10% of RHA of the cement (Ahsan 
and Hossain, 2018). 
 
Experimental work on studying the properties of mortar and 
concrete using rise husk ash as partial substitution of cement 
revealed that RHA consumes more water than concrete without 
RHA. Also, this replacement of cement up to 10% of RHA is 
acceptable (Christopher et al., 2017). Another study showed 
that 25% of the RHA can be used in concrete as a substitute for 
cement, giving the same results of concrete without RHA and 
reduced environmental damages (Khan et al. 2012). Kishore et 
al. (2011) performed experimental work for studying 
mechanical behaviour of the concrete by replacing cement wit 
RHA with the proportion of 0, 5, 10 and 15 percent while M40 
and M50 grade mix of RHA was prepared to determine 
compressive strength on 7, 28 and 56 days.  From this 
experimental work, the researchers concluded that the 
workability is reduced to 27% when RHA is added while 
optimal use of RHA in achieving sufficient strength of the 
concrete is 10%. Beyond this, the concrete gradually loses its 
compressive strength.  The use of RHA is concrete also has a 
positive effect showing the resistance to Sulphate attacks. This 
was demonstrated through experimental work carried out by 
adding RHA with cement as a partial replacement, where it was 
found that concrete using RHA up to 20% exerts substantial 
resistance to sulphate attack (Jaya et al. 2018). However, many 
researchers have argued that use of RHA in concrete consumes 
more water than OPC based concrete. Hence a study focused on 
assessing w/c ratio in concrete using RHA together with OPC 
by testing 150 samples and it was reported that optimal 
water/binder ratio is 0.50 to 0.55 and an optimal amount of

have reported different dosages suitable for the replacement of 
cement with RHA achieving sufficient strength and improved 
workability. Rasoul et al. (2017) cited that Isaia et al. (2003) 
discovered 50% RHA substituting cement having the identical 
strength value to that of ordinary concrete. Still, Ganesan et al. 
(2008) proposed the maximum replacement level of OPC with 
RHA to be 30% beyond which deterioration in the concrete 
performance was expected. Another researcher Ettu et al. (2013) 
limited the RHA dose only at 15% based on his study and the 
type of RHA used and concrete produced then. Whereas, 
Safiuddin (2010), optimized the RHA dosage ranging between 
10% to 20%. It is worth noting that a bare minimum value of 
RHA being 5% only is also recommended by Leong (2015) for 
the improved strength of concrete. Even a few researchers have 
reported adverse effect of RHA on concrete’s strength 
irrespective of the dosage. At any dosage, the decreased 
strength compared to normal concrete is reported by 
Madandoust et al. (2011) and Marthong (2012). This variation 
in the optimal value of RHA dosage has been attributed to many 
factors but the dominating factors are outlined as; fineness of 
the silica particles resulting in their huge surface area and the 
particle properties such as shapes (spherical to irregular ratio) 
(Safiuddin et al., 2010). 
 
Based on above literature review, it can be concluded that the 
Rice Husk Ash (RHA) is obtained by the burning of ‘Rice 
Husk’, an agricultural waste of Rice crop during rice 
processing. RHA possesses siliceous properties which tends it 
to be a cementitious material. Likewise, other cementitious 
materials, its effectiveness depends on many factors such as the 
source of the Husk, the burning temperature, fineness of the 
particles, replacement level of cement with RHA, type of 
concrete, water-binder ration, curing regime and age etc. It is, 
therefore, the literature reports a lot of discrepancies and 
staggering results based on the studies carried by different 
researchers. Pakistan is an agricultural country producing rice 
crop as one of the main crops. Thus, a substantial amount of 
rice husk is also produced during the rice processing. This husk 
either is used as fuel in the rice mills, leaving rice husk ash 
again causing environmental issues or husk directly is dumped 
in open areas, leading to the scarcity of the dumping space and 
environmental problems. This led to the idea to undertake the 
experimental study to understand the effect of rice husk ash by 
burning the husk in the laboratory and husk obtained from the 
local areas. For this study, rice husks were collected from 
Nawabshah city.

5. CONCLUSION 
Experimental investigations carried on the concrete with RHA 
in terms of workability and average compressive strength are 
concluded as under. 
 
 
The addition of RHA as supplementary binding material 
changes both the properties studied; workability and 
compressive strength. 
 
 
The workability of concrete was measured by adopting 
slump cone test and was measured in millimetres (mm). 
Workability of concrete due to RHA decreases in all the 
cases of the RHA replacement level. The decrease was not 
proportional to the increase in the dosage.  
 
 
A smaller dosage the extent of decrease was low as 
compared to the drastic decrease in the workability beyond 
the 15% of RHA in concrete. it almost reached to non 
workable concrete at 25% and 30% RHA dosage. 
 
 
The maximum workability obtained was 64mm in ordinary 
concrete without RHA and it reached only 7mm for 
concrete with 30% RHA. This shows that the addition of 
RHA in concrete should be carried carefully in terms of the 
workability and necessary admixtures like plasticizers may 
be used to achieve minimum required workability of 
concrete. 
 
 
The average compressive strength of concrete of ordinary 
concrete at 28 days was found to be maximum being 24.63 
MPa and that of the same concrete and at same age with 
5% and 10% RHA was observed to be 24.43 MPa and 
24.47 MPa respectively. The difference is negligible being 
1.4% and 0.7% only. This infers that RHA may be used as 
cement replacement in concrete up to 10% by weight of 
cement.  
 
 
Beyond 10%  of RHA substantial decrease is observed in 
average compressive strength being 14.5%, 37.8%, 42.8% 
and 45.9% in case of RHA replacement being 15%, 20%, 
25% and 30% respectively. However, it is worth noting 
that, the concrete with RHA 30% is also applicable in 
relatively low strength applications.  Also during this study 
workability was maintained because of not using ay 
plasticizer. It is expected that keeping all other aspects 
same, if plasticizer be used to maintain the workability, the 
compressive strength may be reaching to that of ordinary 
concrete. This aspect needs to be investigated in future.  
 
 
On the basis of the experimental study carried out and the 
results cited above, it may be concluded that, the 10% 
RHA may be considered as optimum level without 
compromising both the aspects studied i.e workability and 
average compressive strength of concrete which were 
almost equal to those of the ordinary concrete without 
RHA.
