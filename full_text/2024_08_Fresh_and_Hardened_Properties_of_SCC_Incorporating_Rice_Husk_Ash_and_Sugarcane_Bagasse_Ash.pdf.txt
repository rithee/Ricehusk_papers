Abstract. Self-compacting concrete represents a unique type of concrete 
that achieves self-compression through its mass, eliminating the need for 
external vibrations. The mechanical characteristics of self-compacting 
concrete can be enhanced by incorporating agricultural waste materials as 
partial substitutes for cement. In this experimental study, a graded M30 
concrete mix was employed to produce self-compacting concrete by 
replacing cement with 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14% of sugarcane bagasse ash and 
rice husk ash. To assess the concrete's properties, both in its fresh and 
hardened states, several tests were conducted. Fresh properties were 
evaluated using l-box, slump flow, and U-box tests. The results indicated 
that sugarcane bagasse ash outperformed rice husk ash in enhancing the 
fresh properties of concrete up to a certain level of replacement. Hardened 
properties, including split tensile strength, compressive strength, and 
flexural strength, were tested at both 7-day and 28-day intervals. These tests 
revealed that rice husk ash exhibited superior performance in enhancing the 
hardened properties compared to sugarcane bagasse ash. In conclusion, this 
study underscores the suitability of both sugarcane bagasse ash and rice husk 
ash as viable options for partial cement replacement in self-compacting 
concrete, with each material exhibiting strengths in improving certain 
aspects of fresh and hardened concrete properties. 
1 Introduction 
Self-compacting concrete (SCC) is a type of concrete that can compact itself by its weight. It 
was first used in 1988 in Japan. Self-compacting concrete is used where the structure is very 
dense and it is very difficult to apply the vibrators. These days SCC is used where the need 
for high-performance concrete with a structure height between 12m to 15m is known as 
midrise structure. In this type of structure mainly grade M30 is used [1,2]. The current 
research is based on introducing SCC features into normal concrete mixes, with the 
incorporation of ash from agricultural wastes such as Sugarcane bagasse ash (BA) and Rice 
husk ash (RHA).

The surge in industrialization in India has resulted in a population increase, contributing to 
elevated food waste and the subsequent rise in the utilization of by-products like bagasse ash 
[2]. 
  
The fresh properties of a concrete mix are influenced by the use of chemical 
admixtures, such as superplasticizers and viscosity-modifying agents, commonly employed 
in standard concrete mixes. However, there is a noticeable absence of studies that investigate 
the incorporation of agricultural waste materials like Rice Husk Ash (RHA) and Bagasse Ash 
(BA) into normal self-compacting concrete to assess their impact on fresh properties. 
Sugarcane Bagasse ash and Rice husk ash operate as the VMA (Viscosity Modified Agent). 
This is due to the Viscosity Modified Agent admixture, which is low and costlier, thereby 
increasing the cost of manufacturing. SCC, BA, and RHA are all considered normal strength 
suited for replacing VMAs in SCC preparation. In light of the foregoing, the main focus of 
this paper is to observe the workability of self-compacting concrete through limited 
replacement of cement with sugarcane Bagasse ash and Rice husk ash to determine the best 
magnitudes of replacement. 
The disposal challenge of sugarcane bagasse, a significant by-product of the sugar 
production industry, is exacerbated by its environmental impacts and health concerns [2]. 
The leading purpose of this study is to define the fresh and hardened properties of SCC with 
the fractional replacement of cement with RHA at different replacement levels to reduce the 
cost of concrete mix and use agricultural waste in concrete. The replacement is at 0, 5, 10, 
15, and 20% by mass of the cement. While the RHA % increases, the fresh properties of 
concrete decrease in flowability, and the hardening properties are increased by 11.8% in 
compression at 10% and 7.8% in split tensile at the same 10% replacement level [3]. 
In a study about the partial replacement of BA as fine aggregate at the replacement level of 
0-40%, the author conducted 7 days and 28-day tensile strength and compressive tests. The 
water-binder ratio is taken as 0.50, and the mix proportion is M15. He found that the slump 
increased by increasing Sugarcane bagasse ash, and the 28-days compressive strength and 
tensile strength increased by 7.90% and 14% at 10% addition of SCBA [4]. The author 
conducted a study in which he replaced the SCBA with fine aggregate of 0 to 40 levels, and 
he found that the flexural strength is improved by 14.41 at 10% of replacement, and the slump 
is reduced with the rise of sugarcane bagasse ash [5]. In another study, it is found at 10-30% 
replacement level, but the concrete decreases its flowability decrease with the increase of the 
% of SCBA [2].

2 Properties of Material 
2.1 Cement 
OPC of grade 43, according to the Indian standard IS: 8112 (1989), is chosen for 
experimental purposes. The denoted specific gravity is 3.14. All tests are performed as per 
IS 4031 of 1988 [13-14].  
2.2 Fine Aggregate 
Aggregates procured from a 4.75mm sieve are considered for the research work. Fine 
aggregates with a fineness modulus of 3.05, specific gravity of 2.69, and water absorption of 
1.1% are used for the mix. 
2.3 Coarse Aggregates 
Locally available coarse aggregates sized 10mm to 20mm are used. The specified coarse 
aggregates have a fineness modulus of 7.9 and a specific gravity of 2.74. Water absorption 
of 0.55% is observed.

RHA is obtained from the incineration of rice husk, removed from the paddy during the 
milling process. The burning is carried out under controlled temperatures, maintained above 
800 degrees Celsius. This process yields approximately 25%, with RHA composition being 
around 90% amorphous silica and 5% carbon content. The siliceous content in rice husk ash 
imparts pozzolanic properties to RHA. Silica reacts with Ca(OH)2, producing additional C-
S-H, thereby enhancing concrete strength. RHA serves as an easily available raw material 
from agricultural waste for cement replacement.  
2.5 Bagasse Ash 
Bagasse ash is generated by burning sugarcane bagasse in sugar manufacturing factories or 
industries. Similar to fly ash, bagasse ash is black due to the presence of unburnt carbon. In 
contrast to cement, bagasse ash possesses a lower specific gravity, measured at 2.43. The 
particle size of bagasse ash is predominantly less than 85 microns, limiting its potential use 
and making landfill disposal the primary option. Bagasse ash is enriched with silica dioxide, 
promoting increased pozzolanic activity in concrete. Bagasse ash yields silica at about 80%, 
with a 73% absorption of moisture.

3.3 Methodology

study, a graded mix of M30 concrete has been used to prepare self-compacting concrete with 
cement replacement using SCBA and RHA. The prepared concrete mix is compared with 
normal SCC without any replacement. The concrete is prepared according to the proportions 
given in Table 3.2. The concrete is prepared by dry mixing the required materials in a 
machine mixture until a uniform mix is achieved. Then, water mixed with the required super-
plasticizer is added to the mix, and concrete is prepared. Firstly, the prepared concrete is 
tested in a fresh state for the fresh properties of concrete using three methods: L-box, slump 
flow, and v-funnel tests. For the hardened properties of concrete, several samples of cubes, 
cylinders, and beams are cast for the split tensile strength, compressive strength, and flexural 
strength of concrete. Samples cast are opened after 24 hours and placed in a curing pond for

4 Result 
4.1 Fresh Properties of SCC 
4.1.1 Slump Flow 
Slump flow assesses the flow ability of self-compacting concrete under gravitational action, 
minimizing external forces to zero for compaction effects. Previous studies in this field 
achieved a slump value of 550mm, whereas our well-precised test, conducted 2 to 3 seconds 
after mixing, falls under the T500 slump flow. The values are illustrated in Fig. 4.1. 
 
 
Fig. 4.1. Variation in Slump Flow Values 
From the test results, it is found that normal concrete has a 580mm slump flow, and the BA6 
sample increases slump flow values by 1.72%, while BA8 increases the slump value by 
6.89% compared to normal concrete. However, it is observed that after increasing bagasse 
ash by 10%, 12%, and 14%, the slump flow values gradually decrease compared to normal 
concrete slump test values. Additionally, it is observed that after replacing cement with 8% 
and 10% of rice husk, the slump test results increase by 1.2% and 3.8%, respectively. Bagasse 
ash shows better properties than the normal mix and the rice husk ash replacement. Fig. 4.2 
visually portrays the slump flow characteristics of self-compacting concrete, providing a 
valuable reference for assessing its flow ability and workability in construction scenarios.

4.1.2 L-box 
The L-box test assesses the transient capability of self-compacting concrete by its weight 
without any vibration. This test defines the ability of concrete to pass through confined spaces 
and fine openings, validated with a misnamed ratio of the depth of concrete in the lateral 
section synchronous to the depth of concrete in the longitudinal desertion, which is also 
validated at 0.8. Findings reveal that the normal concrete L-Box ratio is 0.93, and the BA6 
sample has a maximum L-box ratio of 0.96. With an increase in bagasse ash percentage 
replacing cement in SCC, the L-box ratio decreases gradually. Conversely, the RA6 sample 
shows a 0.92 L-box ratio, and increasing rice husk ash percentage instead of cement in SSC 
decreases the L-box ratio, as shown in Fig. 4.3.

4.2 Hardened Properties  
4.2.1 Compressive Strength  
Various cube specimens of size 150X150X150mm are prepared for the compressive strength 
of concrete. For every mix, three samples are cast for the 7th-day strength, and three are for 
the 28th-day strength of concrete. Compressive strength is determined with the help of a 
CTM under a loading rate of 13.7N/mm2 according to BIS: 516–1999 (1959).  
It was observed that when we increase the percentage of sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA), the 
compressive strength increases up to a certain level, as shown in Fig. 4.4. The best result is 
found at 8% of SCBA, which is 33.97MPa; after that, when we increase the percentage of 
SCBA, the compressive strength decreases. A similar trend is observed with Rice Husk Ash 
(RHA). RHA increases the compressive strength up to 10% substitution, reaching 35.79MPa; 
however, further increases in RHA result in a decrease in compressive strength, as shown in 
Fig. 4.5. The outcomes indicate that Rice Husk Ash provides better results than SCBA.

4.2.2 Flexural Strength 
Various beam samples of size 100X100X500mm are prepared for the flexural strength of 
concrete. For every mix, three beams for 7 days and three beams for 28 days are cast for 
flexural strength testing. The flexural test is conducted with the help of a flexural strength 
testing machine with two-point loading. 
It was observed that the highest strength is witnessed at 8% substitution of cement with 
SCBA, reaching 4.45 MPa. With an increase in the percentage of SCBA, the flexural strength 
decreases. Similarly, the highest strength with fractional replacement of RHA is at the 10% 
substitution level, reaching 4.46 MPa; however, further increases in RHA result in a decrease 
in flexural strength. RHA and SCBA show similar results in flexural strength, with RHA 
providing slightly more strength, as shown in Fig. 4.7. Fig. 4.8 in the research paper 
showcases the flexural strength testing for concrete, providing a visual insight into the 
experimental setup employed to assess the material's ability to withstand bending forces.

5 Conclusion 
This experimental study aimed to assess the potential of utilizing agricultural waste materials, 
specifically sugarcane bagasse ash and rice husk ash, as partial substitutes for cement in self-
compacting concrete (SCC). SCC, renowned for its self-compression capabilities, is an 
innovative construction material, and this research sought to enhance its mechanical 
properties through sustainable means. The following conclusions are derived from the 
present study: 
The highest compressive strength values are achieved when 10% of the cement is replaced 
with Rice Husk Ash (RHA). However, increasing the percentage of RHA results in a decrease 
in compressive strength, as depicted in Fig. 4.5.

bagasse ash. The highest value of split tensile strength is at the 10% partially substitution
