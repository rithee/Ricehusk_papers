ABSTRACT
Laboratory investigation was conducted focused on the utilisation of waste
materials comprising Class F fly ash (FA) and rice husk ash (RHA), in roller
compacted concrete (RCC) for pavement applications. The mixtures of RCC
comprised CEM I 52.5 N Portland cement containing 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%
and 30% wt FA or RHA. Various aggregate characteristics were measured
to determine their suitability for road applications. Compaction tests were
done to obtain values of optimum moisture content for RCC mixtures. The
mechanical properties investigated comprised compressive strength, static
modulus of elasticity, dynamic modulus of elasticity and ultrasonic pulse
velocity. Results showed that proportions of up to 5–10% wt RHA or FA
were optimal and can be recommended for incorporation in RCC pavement
mixtures. Standard models giving the relationships between compressive
strength,staticanddynamicelasticmoduliofnormalconcretes,werefound
to be also applicable to RCCs.

1. Introduction
Over the years, roller compacted concrete (RCC) has gained wide acceptance as a viable construc-
tion material used in mass concrete applications, especially dam construction and roadworks. RCC is
generally similar to normal or conventional concrete except for its zero – slump mixture typically pre-
paredathigherfineaggregatecontent,alongwithlowercementandwatercontents.Accordingly,RCC
is a stiff mixture suitable for pavement construction, wherein compaction is done using the asphalt
roller compactor, hence the use of the name ‘roller compacted’. Typically, RCC is capable of carrying
lightvehiculartrafficimmediatelyafterpavementplacement.Unlikeconventionalconcreteroads,RCC
pavements do not involve the embedment of steel reinforcement; however, they are generally dense,
durable and strong with high load-bearing capacity (Hafez et al., 2023; Harrington et al., 2010). The
total aggregate content of RCC pavement mixtures is typically higher, being in the range of 75–85% by
volume compared to 60–75% by volume for normal concrete pavements (ACI, 1995; Harrington et al.,
2010). Construction of RCC pavements does not involve the installation of joints, formwork, dowels
or steel reinforcement, which simplifies practical works, making the application fast and economical
(Harrington et al., 2010).

RCC was reportedly first used in the 1930s for the construction of low-traffic roads in Sweden. In
the 1940s and 1980s, RCC was used for the construction of airport runways and military facilities in the
USA, respectively. This pavement technology has presently gained worldwide acceptance for use in
highway engineering and heavy-duty and light industrial applications (FHWA, 2016; Harrington et al.,
2010). Accordingly, RCC has been used to construct parking or storage areas for heavily loaded trucks
and aircraft, and to construct runways in airports, highway shoulders and local streets, amongst others
(ACPA, 2014). Such RCC applications have been implemented in various countries including the USA,
UK and South Africa, amongst others (CSRA, 1987; Du Plessis et al., 2014; FHWA, 2016; UK Government,
2021).
This study focused on the utilisation of fly ash (FA) and rice husk ash (RHA) in RCC. Both materials are
waste by– products and their utilisation in concrete has several engineering and environmental ben-
efits, along with cost reduction. These pozzolanic materials react with the calcium hydroxide product
arising from cement hydration, to form secondary calcium – silicate – hydrate (C-S-H), thereby reduc-
ing permeability and improving durability, generally. Their pore–infilling effect also promotes the
formation of denser microstructure, thereby enhancing mechanical properties (Fulton’s, 2001; Neville,
1996).
It may be recalled that pozzolanic materials, including FA, RHA, ground granulated blast – furnace
slag (GGBS), silica fume (SF) and others, are already specified in cement standards (EN 197-1, 2000) for
incorporation as supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs). It is also well – established that the
production of Portland cement is responsible for 5–8% CO2 emissions into the atmosphere. As such,
incorporation of SCMs into concrete mixtures contributes to the environmental benefit of reducing
CO2 emissions. Besides, SCMs are known to generally improve the durability properties of concretes
(Fulton’s, 2001; Naghizadeh & Ekolu, 2017; Neville, 1996).
1.1. Roller compacted concrete for pavements
1.1.1. Specifications
The UK national standards for highways, for example, have developed a design chart for estimating
the concrete thickness layer of RCC pavements (National Highways UK, 2021). For quality control, the
minimum compressive strength of 50 or 5 MPa minimum flexural strength, is specified as the basis
for layer thickness design (UK Government, 2021). According to CSRA specification for concrete roads,
a minimum 28–day compressive strength of 38 MPa or corresponding flexural strength of 3.8 MPa,
is considered adequate to provide sufficient load-bearing capacity and abrasion resistance (CSRA,
1987). Generally, the 28–day compressive strength of 28–41 MPa, along with flexural strength values
of 3.5–7 MPa are typically used or specified. It is suggested in the literature that high-performing RCC
pavements should typically attain compressive and flexural strengths of 40 and 4.5 MPa, respectively
(Rambabu et al., 2023; Selvam et al., 2022). RCC pavement construction works can be fully opened to
normal traffic, upon attaining a minimum compressive strength of 13.8–17.2 MPa (Harrington et al.,
2010).
1.1.2. Mixture design
RCC mixtures differ widely from those of conventional concrete. The sand content of normal concrete
mixtures is usually 35–50%, being typically lower than stone content. In contrast, RCC mixtures usually
have higher sand content of about 50–55%, while stone content can be below 40%. As mentioned
earlier, the binder content of RCC mixtures is also lower than that of normal concretes. The mini-
mum cementitious content for RCC pavements is typically 250 kg/m3 or 10%, whereas that of normal
concretes is higher, being typically in the range of 10–15% (FHWA, 2016; Harrington et al., 2010; Ram-
babu et al., 2023). Interestingly, studies have also been done to investigate RCC mixtures of very low
80–160 kg/m3 cementitious content (Villena et al., 2011). Another crucial mix design aspect that dis-
tinguishes RCC from normal concrete, is water content. For normal concrete, water content is a major
variable that is adjusted in mix design along with cement content, to obtain the required workability

and concrete strength. In contrast, the amount of water used in RCC mixtures is not prescribed in a
similar manner as it is done for normal concrete. Rather, the water content used in RCC mixtures is a
fixed quantity determined as the optimum moisture content (OMC) based on the moisture–density
relationship obtained using the soil–compaction approach (ASTM D1557, 2012). During construction,
it is desirable for the pavement to develop enhanced early–age strength or load-bearing capacity,
which subsequently allows early opening of the road to traffic. Accordingly, RCC mixtures are typically
made with continuously – graded combined aggregate, which promotes interlocking of aggregate
particles of different size fractions (FHWA, 2016). Being zero – slump mixtures, the workability of RCCs
is more fittingly considered as consistency and is measured using the Vebe test method (ASTM C1170,
2008). Otherwise, most other mechanical properties of RCC including compressive strength, splitting
tensile strength, modulus of elasticity and others, are measured in a manner similar to those of normal
concrete.
1.2. Roller-compacted concrete containing fly ash or rice husk ash
1.2.1. Influence of fly ash
Most desirable engineering properties of RCC pavements are similar to those of conventional con-
crete pavements including, fresh concrete properties, mechanical properties such as compressive
strength, splitting tensile strength, flexural strength, modulus of elasticity, abrasion resistance, fatigue
behaviour, bond strength and durability – related properties including porosity, water absorption,
alkali-silica reaction, amongst others (ACI, 1995; Hafez et al., 2023; Selvam et al., 2022; Tayabji & Okam-
moto, 1987). It is known from various literatures that incorporation of FA into mixtures positively
impacts RCC properties (Hafez et al., 2023; Mohamad et al., 2024; Solikin et al., 2025).
Rao et al. (2015; 2016) incorporated proportions of 0%, 20%, 40% and 60% FA into RCC mixtures
made at 295 kg/m3 cementitious content. They reported that OMC increased with a rise in FA content,
from 5.7% for the control to 7.1% for the mixture containing 60% FA, while compressive, flexural and
splitting tensile strength values decreased correspondingly.
In the investigation conducted by Mardani-Aghabaglou and Ramyar (2013), proportions compris-
ing 0%, 20%, 40% and 60% FA were incorporated as partial replacements of cement or fine aggregate.
The cementitious content of 250 kg/m3 was used in all mixtures. They reported that partial replace-
ment of cement with FA led to a decrease in compressive, flexural and splitting tensile strength results.
In contrast, the opposite effect was observed for RCC wherein fine aggregate was replaced with FA as
these mixtures exhibited increases in mechanical properties with a rise in the content of the SCM.
Their findings are consistent with those reported by Seav et al. (2023) showing that incorporation of
0%, 25%, 50% and 100% bottom ash as sand replacement, led to an increase in compressive and flex-
ural strength values of the mixtures. Cao et al. (2000) also reported that while RCC mixtures containing
up to 55% FA as sand replacement initially showed lower strength, the results exhibited greater late
strength gain which at 90 days exceeded that of the control.
1.2.2. Influence of rice husk ash
Rice is an important and popular food staple worldwide, more so in populous developing countries
including China, India and Indonesia, amongst others. As such, rice production volume worldwide is
exorbitant, having recently reached about 787 million tonnes per year (FAO, 2022). Each tonne of
rice produces 20% raw husk and 4% RHA as agricultural waste material (Mehta, 1992; Villena et al.,
2011). However, very limited research presently exists on the potential use of RHA in RCC. More-
over, it is necessary to distinguish between the use of RHA for partial cement replacement versus its
use as sand replacement material. Research studies show that incorporation of RHA as fine aggre-
gate in RCC typically increases compressive strength (Meghwar et al., 2024). Such a study by Villena
et al. (2011) found 5–7% RHA to be the optimum proportion for the use of the SCM as sand replace-
ment material in RCC. Vahidi et al. (2017) reported that the use of 9% RHA as cement replacement
material gave optimum compressive strength, but results decreased at SCM proportions above 15%

RHA (Aghaeipour & Madhkhan, 2020). Similar observations, as discussed in the foregoing, have been
reported regarding the use of RHA as cement replacement material in normal concrete, wherein pro-

on cementitious content (Ahmed et al., 2020; Ahmed & Kamau, 2017; Kamau et al., 2018).
In an investigation by Modarres and Hosseini (2014), proportions of 3–5% RHA were incorporated

MDD owing to the use of RHA. Panpipat and Cheerarot (2022) incorporated 0%, 10%, 20% and 30%

They reported that RCC mixtures containing up to 10% RHA gave similar compressive strength values
as the control, but at higher proportions of the SCM, strength decreased correspondingly. Ahmed et al.
(2020) reported similar observations, as in the foregoing, showing that RCC mixtures containing up to
10% wt RHA gave pavement concrete strength that was similar to or higher than that of the control
mixture.
1.3. Objectives and research significance
The objectives of the present study were to investigate the performance responses of RCC mixtures
containing FA which is a by–product from coal–power stations, along with those containing RHA
which is an agricultural waste material. In the present investigation, RCC mixtures were prepared
using CEM I 52.5N Portland cement containing 0–30% wt FA or RHA. Various material characteristics
of aggregates were measured including relative density, water absorption, particle size distribution,
Aggregate Crushing Value (ACV) and Los Angeles Abrasion (LAA). The mechanical properties of RCC
mixtures measured comprised:- compressive strength development, splitting tensile strength, ultra-
sonic pulse velocity (UPV) and static and dynamic moduli of elasticity. As an additional objective,
the standard relationship models for the foregoing mechanical properties of normal concretes were
evaluated for potential applicability to RCCs.
The practical significance of this research is related to the engineering and environmental benefits
of utilising RHA and FA in RCC mixtures, along with a reduction in the cost of pavement construction.
Presently, RCC pavement construction is hardly or not at all employed in most developing countries.
It is, however, well – established that RCC is an economical material system that allows fast pavement
construction and could contribute beneficially to engineering applications. It is also essential to con-
sider that bituminous mixtures are presently the dominant pavement construction material in most
developing countries. As such, employment of RCC for pavement construction not only reduces fossil
fuel consumption since bitumen is a by-product of petroleum, but it would also increase the utilisation
of waste materials such as FA and RHA, thereby promoting sustainability.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Optimum moisture content results
Figure 4(a,b) shows the compaction curves of RCC mixtures, giving the variation in dry density with a
rise in moisture content from 4.5% to 8.5%, under the influence of different FA or RHA proportions. The
OMC of the control mixture was 6.5%. It can be seen in Figure 4(a) that although the curve profiles were
different, the OMC value for MDD was consistently 6.5% regardless of FA proportion, implying that the
SCM had no significant influence on the water content of the RCC mixtures. However, the influence
of RHA was somehow different from that of FA. Figure 4(b) shows that at lower proportions of up to
10% wt RHA, the SCM had no significant effect on OMC, instead it maintained 6.5% water content. But
at 20% and 30% wt RHA, the OMC shifted to the higher water content of 7.5%, implying that the use of
higher proportions of the SCM led to an increase in water demand of the RCC mixtures. Similar findings

However, significant differences can be seen between the effects of FA and those of RHA. It is
evident that the 5–10% wt FA mixtures showed up to 13.6% reduction of early age 7 – day com-
pressive strength, beyond which the mixtures showed late strength gains that caught up to give
similar long–term values as those of the control. In contrast, the 5–10% wt RHA mixtures significantly
enhanced early–age 7 – day compressive strength exhibiting up to a 6.4% increase, but gave reduced
long–term strength relative to that of the control. The tendency exhibited by FA is typical of pozzolans
that possess significant quantities of both silica and alumina, being usually within the range of 12–40%
SiO2 and 30–60% Al2O4. Such SCMs comprise natural pozzolans including volcanic ash, metakaolin,
zeolite or artificial pozzolans such as FA, GGBS and others (Ekolu et al., 2006; Naghizadeh & Ekolu, 2017;
Sinngu et al., 2022; 2023).
Meanwhile, silica–rich SCMs tend to be more effective in enhancing early strength but do not
exhibit a significant effect on long–term strength growth. Among conventional SCMs of this cate-
gory is SF, which is known for enhancing early strength, especially for high-strength concretes. SF
typically contains > 90% SiO2 content, and is usually incorporated into concrete at low proportions
of about 5–15% wt (Fulton’s, 2001; Nochaiya et al., 2010). Similarly, the RHA used in the present study
was a silica–rich pozzolan containing a high 89.2% SiO2 content (Table 1), which underscores its early
– strength-enhancing effect on RCC, as seen for the 5–10% wt RHA mixtures (Figure 5(b)).
Figure 6 compares the compressive strengths of RCC mixtures containing FA versus those contain-
ing RHA. It can be seen that the strength values of 5–10% wt RHA mixtures, were generally similar to
those of corresponding FA mixtures. But upon a rise in SCM proportions to higher levels of 20–30% wt,
the performance of RHA mixtures significantly diminished to levels lower than those of the corre-
sponding FA mixtures. Indeed, strength values of the 20% and 30% wt RHA mixtures were respectively

content, once the calcium hydroxide arising from cement hydration has been depleted due to poz-

strength, merely fulfilling the role of a filler (Ahmed et al., 2020).

effective at proportions not exceeding 10% wt. This finding is consistent with those in the various lit-

concretes (Amran et al., 2021; Bhowmik & Pal, 2023; Fitriani et al., 2022). Evidently, RCC mixtures con-

3.3. Splitting tensile strength results
Figure 7 shows the 28–day splitting tensile strength results of RCC mixtures containing FA and RHA.
Evidently, the tensile strength values of both pozzolans reduced with a rise in SCM content, as similarly
seen for compressive strength results (Figure 5). Interestingly, the reductions in tensile strength results
of FA mixtures were consistently within 16–23% regardless of the SCM proportion. In contrast, RHA
mixtures exhibited a steady decline in tensile strength values with a rise in SCM proportion from 0% to
30% wt. A drastic drop in tensile strength results occurred at the higher proportions of 20–30% wt RHA,
giving reductions of 50–60%. These observations indicate the absence of significant late strength gain
in RCC mixtures containing RHA, whereas the mixtures containing FA exhibited long–term strength
growth attributed to pozzolanic reaction, thereby attaining significant tensile strength values even
at higher proportions of the SCM (Figure 7). It is well – established that SCMs densify the interfacial
transition zone (ITZ), a relatively porous and weak microstructural aggregate–paste interface typically
prone to crack initiation during concrete failure (Nduka et al., 2022). The splitting tensile strength val-
ues of mixtures containing up to 10% wt FA or 10% wt RHA, were all between 2.0 and 2.5 MPa. But at
higher proportions above 10% wt, the FA mixtures maintained similar splitting tensile strength val-
ues averaging about 2.3 MPa, while the corresponding values for RHA mixtures reduced markedly to
about 1.3 MPa. The foregoing contrasting observations can be attributed to different effects of FA and
RHA on ITZ densification, owing to C-S-H formation in mixtures containing the SCMs (Meenakshi et al.,
2019; Nduka et al., 2022; Sadrmomtazi et al., 2018). Expectedly, the localised effect of ITZ densification
due to the pozzolanic reaction, enhances tensile strength more significantly while showing little to
no impact on compressive strength. The excessive paste volume of mixtures containing higher pro-
portions comprising 20–30% wt RHA, may be responsible for the observed drastic reduction in tensile
strengths of the mixtures.

3.5. Ultrasonic pulse velocity and dynamic modulus of elasticity

and other defects or flaws that undermine concrete properties, impact UPV values which in turn cap-

be of satisfactory quality, with higher values exceeding 3500 and above 4000 m/s indicating good
to excellent quality. Values below 3000 m/s show a loss of integrity and accordingly indicate poor
concrete quality (Neville, 1996).

being of excellent quality. However, there was an interesting difference between the effects of FA and
those of RHA. It can be seen in Figure 10(a) that FA mixtures showed no significant decrease in UPV val-

3.6. Limitations of this study
RHA and FA effects on mechanical properties are partly related to the influence of the SCMs on ITZ.
Also strongly influenced by ITZ are durability properties. This aspect is highlighted in Section 3.3, how-
ever, the present study has not focussed on the effects of the SCMs on durability properties, such as
porosity, permeability and resistance to chemical attack processes. Further research is needed to fully
understand the foregoing aspects related to the durability effects of RHA and FA on RCC mixtures.
4. Conclusions
An investigation was done to evaluate and compare the effects of fly ash (FA) and rice husk ash
(RHA), on the mechanical properties of roller-compacted concrete (RCC) for use as pavement construc-
tion material. The two different supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) were incorporated into
RCC mixtures at proportions of 0–30% wt FA or RHA. The water content quantities used to prepare
RCC mixtures were determined using the soil–compaction approach typically employed to obtain
the moisture–dry density relationship. It was found that mixtures containing 5–10% wt RHA or FA
gave optimum performance results, hence this range of proportions can be considered suitable for
incorporation of the SCMs into RCC mixtures. Being waste by-products, several engineering and envi-
ronmental benefits can result from the use of the SCMs in RCC pavements. Subsequently given are
specific findings drawn from the foregone study.
(1) FA showed no effect on water demand, whereas RHA increased the water demand of mixtures
containing higher SCM proportions comprising 20–30% wt.
(2) Incorporation of 5–10% wt RHA into mixtures enhanced the early–age compressive strength,
but showed no significant effect on late strength growth. In contrast, mixtures containing
5–10% wt FA showed early – age strength reduction, but exhibited late–age compressive

(3) RCC mixtures containing up to 10% FA and 10% RHA gave 28–day compressive strengths

values specified for RCC pavement mixtures.
(4) The RCC mixtures containing 5–30% FA, all gave similar values of splitting tensile strength

of elasticity (Ed) is valid for RCCs in the same manner as employed for normal concretes.
