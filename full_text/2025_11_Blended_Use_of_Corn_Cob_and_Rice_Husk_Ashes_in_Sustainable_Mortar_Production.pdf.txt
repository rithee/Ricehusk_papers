Abstract 
The use of agricultural and industrial by-products in sustainable material production 
has become an increasingly attractive strategy for waste management with growing 
environmental awareness about the possible adverse effects of waste materials. 
Therefore, the partial replacement of cement by mass with corn cob ash (CCA) and 
rice husk ash (RHA) to develop sustainable mortar composites was investigated in 
this study. A three-phase experimental program was conducted, including (i) 
pozzolanic activity assessment, (ii) optimization of CCA–RHA mix ratio, and (iii) 
evaluation of mechanical and durability properties at various blended replacement 
levels (5–20% by mass). Key tests such as compressive and flexural strength, XRD, 
TGA, and SEM showed enhanced cementitious performance with 5–10% cement 
replacement. RHA accelerated early pozzolanic reactions, while CCA contributed to 
long-term durability. Microstructural analyses indicated reduced calcium hydroxide 
(CH) content and increased calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) formation. Results show 
that blended agricultural ashes offer effective SCM potential and contribute to 
sustainability by utilizing waste materials. 
 
Keywords: Supplementary Cementitious Materials (SCM), Agricultural Waste, Corn Cob 
Ash, Rice Husk Ash, Pozzolanic Activity

1. Introduction 
 
Cement production remains one of the 
most 
carbon-intensive 
industrial 
processes worldwide, responsible for 
approximately 
5-8% 
of 
global 
anthropogenic 
CO₂ 
emissions 
(Ekincioğlu et al., 2013), and more 
recently reported as 6% based on 
updated global inventories (Tiseo, 2025). 
Its environmental footprint extends 
beyond 
greenhouse 
gas 
emissions, 
encompassing 
particulate 
pollution, 
resource 
depletion, 
and 
significant 
energy consumption. In response to 
these 
challenges, 
research 
into 
supplementary cementitious materials 
(SCMs) has intensified, aiming to reduce 
Portland 
cement 
usage 
while 
maintaining 
or 
enhancing 
the 
mechanical and durability performance 
of cementitious systems. 
 
SCMs come from many sources and are 
grouped into different categories. Early 
studies evaluated the efficacy of natural 
pozzolans—such 
as 
volcanic 
ash, 
diatomaceous earth, and pumice—for 
their silica-rich composition and long-
term durability enhancement. With 
industrialization, by-products like fly 
ash (FA), ground granulated blast 
furnace slag (GGBFS), and silica fume 
(SF) gained importance due to their high 
reactivity and resource optimization 
opportunities, especially in large-scale 
applications. These materials react with 
calcium 
hydroxide 
(CH) 
through 
secondary hydration reactions, forming 
additional calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-
H) gel, which contributes to denser 
microstructures and improved long-
term properties. 
 
Meanwhile, the utilization of waste 
materials as SCMs has emerged as a 
promising 
way 
for 
sustainable 
construction practices, offering both 
environmental benefits and enhanced 
performance 
characteristics. 
More 
recently, the use of agricultural waste-
based SCMs has gained attention, 
particularly 
for 
their 
role 
in 
sustainability, waste valorization, and 
local 
material 
sourcing. 
Notable 
examples include rice husk ash (RHA), 
corn cob ash (CCA), wheat straw ash 
(WSA), and bagasse ash (BA). Among 
them, RHA has been extensively studied 
due to its high amorphous silica content 
(often exceeding 80%) and its ability to 
react rapidly when properly calcined 
and milled. Prior investigations (e.g., 
Bahri et al., 2019; Le et al., 2014; Noori et 
al., 2023; Jayaraman et al., 2023) report 
consistent enhancements in compressive 
and flexural strength, durability, and 
alkali-silica reaction (ASR) resistance at 
replacement levels of 10–15%. 
 
In contrast to RHA, corn cob ash (CCA) 
remains relatively less studied. Its 
chemical 
reactivity 
and 
pozzolanic 
potential depend strongly on calcination 
conditions, 
particle 
fineness, 
and 
agricultural origin. Previous studies 
have demonstrated moderate silica 
content 
and 
delayed 
strength 
development (Kamau et al., 2016; 
Abubakar et al., 2016), with significant 
improvements observed only when 
CCA is calcined at approximately 700 °C 
and finely ground. Under optimal 
conditions, its pozzolanic activity index 
(PAI) (referred to as the strength activity 
index (SAI) 
in 
ASTM 
C311) 
can

approach 
the 
minimum 
threshold 
required for classification as an SCM. 
Replacement levels between 5% and 15% 
provide 28-day compressive strengths 
comparable to or exceeding control 
mixes, with a 10% replacement offering 
optimal strength gains and improved 
ductility. 
Nevertheless, 
when 
used 
alone, CCA may adversely affect early-
age strength and workability due to its 
relatively low reactivity and high alkali 
content; moreover, dosages above 15% 
lead 
to 
increased 
water 
demand, 
extended setting times, and, in excess of 
20%, often require prolonged curing to 
achieve design strength (Adebisi et al., 
2019). 
 
While both RHA and CCA have 
individually 
demonstrated 
SCM 
viability, their combined use remains 
largely 
unexplored. 
Most 
previous 
investigations have examined rice husk 
ash (RHA) and corn cob ash (CCA) as 
supplementary cementitious materials 
independently, 
leaving 
limited 
understanding of how their combined 
utilization might balance early- and 
later-age reactivity while mitigating 
challenges such as alkali–silica reaction 
and early-age strength reduction. 
 
A few studies (Kumari et al., 2018; 
Olaniyi, 2022) suggest that blending 
these ashes could yield synergistic 
effects in binder design, leveraging 
RHA’s early-age reactivity and CCA’s 
long-term 
strength 
contribution. 
However, 
prior 
research 
lacks 
standardized 
experimental 
comparisons, optimization of blend 
ratios, 
and 
the 
comprehensive 
evaluation of mechanical, durability, 
and microstructural performance. This 
study aims to address that gap through 
a 
comprehensive 
three-phase 
experimental program.  
 
Phase I evaluates the pozzolanic activity 
indices of RHA and CCA across varying 
calcination 
temperatures. 
Phase 
II 
investigates multiple RHA:CCA blend 
ratios to determine the most favorable 
combination 
for 
mechanical 
performance. Phase III applies this 
optimum 
blend 
to 
mortars 
with 
replacement levels of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 
20%, assessing fresh state properties 
(workability, 
air 
content), 
strength 
development, 
durability 
(ASR 
expansion, capillary absorption), and 
microstructural changes through XRD, 
TGA, and SEM analyses. 
 
By improving our understanding of 
blended agricultural waste pozzolans, 
this study aims to contribute to the 
development of cementitious systems 
that are low in carbon and efficient in 
resource use, contributing to global 
sustainability efforts. 
 
2. Materials and Methods 
 
This study investigates the pozzolanic 
performance of corn cob ash (CCA) and 
rice husk ash (RHA) in mortar systems 
through a three-phase experimental 
program. 
All 
procedures 
were 
conducted in accordance with relevant 
ASTM and EN standards. 
 
2.1. Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) 
 
The cement used was CEM I 42.5N type 
Portland cement, classified according to

BS EN 197-1, and its density was 
measured as 3.09 g/cm3. 
 
2.2. Fine Aggregate 
 
Natural sand used in the mixtures was 
obtained from the Sakarya River and 
sieved through a 2 mm sieve to eliminate 
oversized particles before mixing. The 
density was determined as 2.66 g/cm³, 
and particle size distribution conformed 
to the grading requirements specified in 
BS EN 196-1. 
 
2.3. Superplasticizer 
 
To compensate for the increased water 
demand of pozzolanic materials, a 
polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer 
(MasterGlenium 51) was used at dosages 
up to 2% by cement weight, in 
accordance with BS EN 934-2. The 
density of the used superplasticizer was 
measured as 1.1 g/cm³.  
 
2.4. Rice Husk Ash (RHA) 
 
RHA was commercially obtained from 
Erdoğanlar Gıda San. ve Tic. Ltd. Şti., 
having been previously calcined via 
industrial processes.  Before use, the ash 
was oven-dried and ground in a ball mill 
to achieve a fineness below 160 µm, 
suitable for mortar production. The 
RHA used in the study is shown in 
Figure 1, and its density was measured 
as 2.57 g/cm3. 
 
2.5. Corn Cob Ash (CCA) 
 
CCA was used after burning and 
grinding entirely in the laboratory. Corn 
cobs collected from Sakarya Province as 
agricultural 
waste 
material 
were 
calcined at four different temperatures 
(400°C, 500°C, 600°C, and 700°C) for 2 
hours in a muffle furnace to determine 
the optimum burning condition. Then, 
the obtained ash was rapidly cooled with 
water to promote the formation of an 
amorphous structure. After cooling, 
samples were oven-dried at 150°C for 48 
hours and subsequently ground using a 
ball mill to obtain particles finer than 160 
µm. The used CCA is shown in Figure 2, 
and its density was measured as 2.40 
g/cm3.

The high SiO₂ content in RHA (84.88%)

The combined content of SiO₂, Al₂O₃, 
and Fe₂O₃ (S+A+F) exceeds 85% for

pozzolanic 
activity, while CCA shows a value

S+A+F content of CCA tends to increase 
with higher calcination temperatures, 
supporting its potential when thermally 
optimized. This supports the hypothesis 
that RHA promotes early calcium

while CCA contributes to later-age 
hydrates, although at a slower rate.

relatively 
high 
loss 
on

(LOI=11.29%), this is expected due to 
residual organics and moisture. LOI 
values 
are 
known 
to 
decrease 
significantly 
upon 
incineration 
at 
elevated temperatures, which enhances 
the 
ash’s 
reactivity 
and 
stability. 
Therefore, CCA in this study was 
considered as a supplementary material 
only after calcination, particularly in 
blended systems with RHA, where it 
may contribute to long-term matrix 
refinement and promote sustainable 
material utilization.  
 
Low CaO levels in both ashes (1.05% in 
RHA, 2.45% in CCA) confirm their non-
cementitious nature, reinforcing their 
role as supplementary cementitious 
materials. The high K₂O content in CCA 
(39.70%) may influence alkali–silica 
reaction (ASR) risk, while low SO₃ levels 
in both ashes minimize concerns about 
sulfate-related expansion. 
 
These compositional characteristics form 
the basis for selecting and interpreting 
subsequent tests, including SAI, ASR, 
capillary absorption, 
microstructural 
analysis, and mechanical strengths.

Activity Index (PAI)

calcined 
at

temperatures.

2.7.2. Phase II –Optimization of RHA 
and CCA Blend Ratio 
 
In the second phase of the investigation, 
the 
focus 
shifted 
from 
individual 
evaluation to exploring the combined 
effect of CCA and RHA. For this 
purpose, CCA with the highest PAI

measured on the broken halves of 
mortars after flexural testing. All flexural 
and 
compressive 
strength 
values 
represent the average of three specimens 
per mix design, tested to ensure 
consistency and reduce variability in 
reported results.

2.8. Microstructural Analysis 
 
Microstructural 
characterization 
employed the following methods to 
investigate 
the 
pozzolanic 
reaction 
mechanisms, 
including 
calcium 
hydroxide (CH) consumption, calcium 
silicate hydrate (C-S-H) formation, and 
quartz formation as a function of 
increasing cement replacement with 
CCA and RHA.  
 
2.8.1. X-ray Diffraction (XRD) 
 
Five mortar mixtures (M0, M5, M10, 
M15, and M20) were analyzed at the 
Prof. Dr. Adnan Tekin Material Sciences 
and 
Production 
Technologies 
Application Research Center (ATUM) at 
Istanbul 
Technical 
University. 
Specimens were ground to powder, and 
the diffraction data were collected in the 
10–60° 2θ range, where the most 
resolved 
peaks 
were 
observed. 
Quantitative 
phase 
analyses 
were 
performed using computer software 
(Malvern Panalytical ver. 5.2) to assess 
the evolution of hydration products. 
 
2.8.2. 
Thermogravimetric 
Analysis 
(TGA) 
 
Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was 
performed on the same five mixtures 
(M0-M20) to quantify calcium hydroxide 
content through decomposition profiles. 
The samples were ground to a fine 
powder and placed in ceramic crucibles. 
Heating was performed from room 
temperature to 650 °C at a rate of 10 
°C/min under static air conditions. 
Weight loss between 400–600°C was 
used to estimate CH decomposition, 
providing insight into the extent of 
pozzolanic reaction.

(SEM) 
 
SEM imaging was carried out at the 
Membrane Technologies Application-

3. Results and Discussion 
 
3.1. Results of Phase I – Evaluation of 
Pozzolanic Activity Index (PAI) 
 
The pozzolanic performance of as-
received RHA and CCA, calcined at four 
different temperatures (400°C, 500°C, 
600°C, 700°C), was evaluated based on 
compressive strength measurements of 
mortars at 7 and 28 days. Each modified 
mix 
contained 
20% 
pozzolanic 
replacement by weight of OPC. The 
reference mortar (100% OPC was used as 
binder) served as the control specimen 
for calculating PAI values. The results 
are presented in Table 3, showing the 
compressive 
strength 
and 
corresponding PAI values at both curing 
ages. 
 
The data clearly show that, all CCA-
based mixes exhibited relatively low PAI 
values, particularly at early ages, with 
most failing below the ASTM C618 
minimum 
threshold 
of 
0.75. 
This 
indicates limited pozzolanic activity of 
CCA, especially during the initial 
hydration period. Among the CCA 
variants, only the sample calcined at 
700°C 
(CCA700) 
approached 
the 
threshold at both 7 and 28 days (PAI = 
0.75 and 0.76, respectively), suggesting 
improved reactivity due to increased 
amorphous silica content and reduced 
residual organics. This trend aligns with 
findings 
by 
Olaleye 
(2022), 
who 
demonstrated that corncob ash calcined 
at 700°C can serve as a viable pozzolanic 
material 
in 
concrete 
applications. 
Additional support is provided by 
Olafusi 
et 
al. 
(2018), 
whose 
characterization of CCA calcined at 
approximately 625–650°C confirmed its 
compliance with ASTM C618 criteria 
and its suitability as a cementitious 
additive. 
 
In contrast, RHA exhibited higher PAI 
values (0.89 at 7 days and 0.83 at 28 
days), 
confirming 
its 
superior 
pozzolanic performance, particularly at 
early ages. This behavior is attributed to 
its highly reactive amorphous silica 
phase 
and 
fine 
particle 
size, as 
supported by Siddika et al. (2020), who 
reported that ash fineness and the 
presence 
of 
amorphous 
silica 
significantly accelerate early C–S–H 
formation and CH consumption, thereby 
enhancing pozzolanic reactivity. 
 
These 
findings 
highlight 
the 
temperature- and age-dependent nature 
of CCA’s reactivity and support the 
selection 
of 
CCA700 
for 
further 
investigation in Phase II. Although

days, the observed trends suggest

3.2. Results of Phase II – Optimization 
of RHA/CCA Blend Ratio 
 
In Phase II, the total replacement level 
was fixed at 20% by mass, by varying the 
internal ratio between RHA and CCA: 
0C20R, 5C15R, 10C10R, 15C5R, and 
20C0R. The objective was to determine 
the 
blend 
that 
offered 
the 
best 
mechanical 
performance 
while 
maintaining acceptable workability. The 
reference mix (M0), consisting entirely of 
OPC as binder, was included for 
comparison under an identical water-to-
binder ratio (w/b = 0.50). The mechanical 
performance results are summarized in 
Table 4, showing the flexural and 
compressive strength results at 7 and 28 
days for all mixes. 
 
As shown in Table 4, all modified mixes 
exhibited lower strength values than the 
reference (M0), reflecting the expected 
impact of partial replacement of OPC. 
Among the alternatives, 0C20R and 
15C5R yielded the highest compressive 
strength at 28 days (49.4 MPa and 49.2 
MPa, respectively), with 15C5R also 
demonstrating 
consistent 
early-age 
performance. Notably, the 20C0R mix, 
containing only CCA, exhibited the 
lowest strength values at both ages, 
suggesting that increasing CCA content 
beyond 15% may adversely affect 
mechanical performance.  
 
Flexural strength results followed a 
similar trend. At 28 days, the blended 
mixes containing both RHA and CCA—
namely 5C15R, 10C10R, and 15C5R—
achieved flexural strengths of 8.3, 8.2, 
and 
8.1 
MPa, 
respectively,

4. Conclusions 
 
The mechanical and microstructural 
performance 
of 
cement 
mortars 
incorporating corn cob ash (CCA) and 
rice husk ash (RHA) as blended 
supplementary cementitious materials 
(SCMs) was investigated in this study. A 
three-phase experimental program was 
conducted to assess pozzolanic activity, 
optimize blend ratios, and evaluate 
performance across varying replacement 
levels. Based on the findings, the 
following conclusions can be drawn: 
• 
Pozzolanic 
Reactivity: 
RHA 
exhibited high pozzolanic activity 
due to its amorphous silica content 
and 
industrial-grade 
fineness, 
contributing to early-age strength 
and 
matrix 
densification. 
CCA 
showed limited reactivity at lower 
calcination 
temperatures 
but 
reached 
acceptable 
pozzolanic 
activity index (PAI) values when 
calcined at 700 °C, justifying its 
inclusion in blended systems. 
• 
Blend Optimization: The 15C5R 
blend (75% CCA + 25% RHA within 
the 
replacement 
fraction) 
demonstrated the most balanced 
mechanical performance, combining 
early-age strength retention with 
long-term durability. This blend was 
selected for further evaluation in 
Phase III. 
• 
Mechanical Performance: Mortars 
with 5–10% total binder replacement 
using the optimal blend retained 92–
97% 
of 
the 
reference 
(M0) 
compressive strength at 28 days. 
Even at 20% replacement, the mix 
achieved 49.2 MPa, indicating its 
suitability 
for 
general-purpose 
applications. 
Flexural 
strength 
followed a similar trend, with 
moderate 
reductions 
remaining 
within 
acceptable 
engineering 
limits. 
• 
Durability 
Indicators: 
Capillary 
water absorption increased with 
higher replacement levels, reflecting 
a more porous matrix due to 
reduced cement content. However, 
ASR expansion values decreased 
from 0.34% to 0.28%, suggesting 
partial 
mitigation 
through 
the 
combined chemical and physical 
effects of RHA and CCA. While this 
reduction suggests improved alkali 
binding matrix densification, the 
expansions still remain above the 
acceptable 
threshold 
of 
0.10%. 
Therefore, the blended ashes show 
promise for durability enhancement, 
but further long-term studies are 
recommended. 
• 
Microstructural Evolution: XRD, 
TGA, and SEM analyses confirmed 
progressive consumption of calcium

hydroxide 
(CH) 
and 
increased 
formation of calcium silicate hydrate 
(C–S–H) with rising SCM content. 
SEM images revealed enhanced 
morphological 
characteristics, 
including reduced CH crystals, 
improved C–S–H continuity, and 
refined pore structure—particularly 
in M15 and M20 mixtures. 
• 
Sustainability Implications: The 
use of agricultural waste ashes not 
only 
reduces

improvement while contributing to 
sustainable 
construction 
practices.  
Future 
research 
should 
focus 
on

and
